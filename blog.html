Why We Need to Let Polymorphism Forget Itself

Today, let's dig into something that’s been on my mind for a while now: polymorphism in object-oriented programming. On the surface, polymorphism sounds like a godsend—letting a single interface control a variety of underlying actions. But lately, I've started wondering if we’re leaning on it a bit too much, to the detriment of our system designs.

This line of thought was sparked by a discussion on how polymorphism is implemented in languages like Python or Java. You know the drill: method overriding, using interfaces, treating different objects the same way. It's all very elegant—until it isn't.

In practice, I've seen polymorphism lead engineers down a path of abstraction that turns into a minefield of maintenance nightmares. When every class and method feels like it's wearing a mask, you start to forget what's actually doing the work. This isn't just a philosophical concern; it's a practical one. We lose sight of what's really happening in the code—especially when it comes to debugging or extending the system under tight deadlines.

It reminds me of race conditions in multi-threaded applications. Race conditions occur when multiple threads access and modify shared data simultaneously, leading to unpredictable outcomes. The parallels to polymorphism here are striking: both involve subtle, often unintended interactions across parts of a system that can be maddeningly difficult to trace when something goes wrong.

So, what are the alternatives? Well, one thing that’s worked in the past for me is to embrace a bit more explicitness in our code. Think about the costs of abstraction and whether the benefits truly outweigh them. Sometimes, it’s better to have a little redundancy for the sake of clarity. Clarity is maintainability’s best friend.

Then there’s the question of whether our concurrency models, like those in Go, offer a lesson here. Go's goroutines and channels suggest a different approach: instead of hiding complexity under layers of abstraction, Go encourages us to manage complexity through well-defined, explicit communication. What if we designed object-oriented systems with a similar mindset? Imagine polymorphism that doesn't try to hide everything behind abstractions but instead makes the interactions crystal clear.

This brings me to a fascinating story I came across: An amateur armed with ChatGPT solves an Erdős problem. The essence here isn't just about solving a mathematical problem but also about how using AI tools made the process more transparent and accessible. What if we approached our codebases this way, with tools that augment our understanding rather than obscure it?

At the core of this is a simple idea: maybe it's time to let polymorphism forget a bit of its own abstraction. Let's embrace explicitness where it counts, use polymorphism judiciously, and keep our systems predictable and maintainable.

As for what I’m still pondering: how do we strike the right balance between abstraction and clarity in large-scale systems? How do we know when we’ve abstracted too much, to the point where our own tools become a black box?
