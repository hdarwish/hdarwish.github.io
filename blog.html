Busywork is Killing Your Codebase

I stumbled upon an article about workplace productivity. It hit me: our obsession with looking busy is sabotaging real innovation.

In tech, the illusion of productivity often overshadows actual results. Endless meetings, constant Slack notifications, and emails that demand immediate responses create a facade of activity.

This isn't productivity. It's theater.

Every morning, I see engineers in the office early. They’re tapping away furiously during standup. It looks impressive. But are they solving complex problems, or just rehearsing for the next status update?

Back when I was new, I thought the key to recognition was constant activity. My GitHub activity graph was full of commits. But meaningful progress? Not as much.

The reality dawned on me when a senior engineer pulled me aside. He said, "Hafs, it's not the hours you sit at your desk. It's the impact of your work." That was a wake-up call.

Think about the last time you sat in a lengthy meeting that was more about appearances than outcomes. How much of your valuable focus did it steal?

In a past role, I joined a project already a year behind schedule. The team was running in circles. They appeared busy, but they were trapped in a cycle of rewriting specs, shifting priorities, and firefighting bug reports.

It was chaos. But upper management saw the team’s activity as a sign of dedication. No one realized the true issue: a flawed process.

We restructured the team around clear objectives rather than random tasks. The noise dropped. Suddenly, we were delivering features, not just reports.

There's a systemic flaw in how success is measured in tech environments. Activity is easy to observe. Output requires real scrutiny.

Performance reviews often reward hours worked over problems solved. Promotions go to those who "seem engaged." Our tools don't help either. Metrics that track keystrokes or time in apps perpetuate the busywork trap.

This illusion of productivity can crush creativity. It discourages downtime — the time when real innovation happens. You can't brute force brilliance; you need space to think.

True productivity isn't about the volume of code, but its quality. It's not the number of meetings attended, but the problems resolved.

Meaningful productivity might mean fewer lines of code. Simpler solutions. Less churn.

I've seen teams pivot to minimal meetings and async communication. Output skyrocketed. More product shipped, less stress endured.

Yet the business world is addicted to appearances. Busy calendars. Full inboxes.

We need to recalibrate. Measure what matters. Celebrate the engineers who ask "Why?" more than "How quickly?"

Recognize that the quiet moments — the ones that don't make noise — are where the magic happens.

A few years ago, we were deep into a refactor of a legacy system. The codebase was a tangled mess. We threw bodies at it. More engineers, more busywork. Yet, the bug count didn't drop.

Then someone suggested we halt all new feature work for a sprint. Focus on understanding and untangling just one critical module. It wasn't flashy. But our system's p95 latency went from 2 seconds to 500ms after that sprint. One sprint. That taught me volumes.

Are you writing software or just scripts for the theater of productivity?

Let go of the busywork. Focus on impact. Real innovation needs space to breathe.

Reframe productivity as the art of doing less but achieving more. That's where the future lies.

Resource management is another lesson. The memory and processing power available in the 90s were minimal by today's standards. Yet, developers created expansive worlds and engaging gameplay. It's a testament to efficient code and creative problem-solving.

These aren't just technical details; they're stories worth preserving. Losing a piece of software history means losing insight into the human ingenuity that got us here.

Reverse-engineering old game servers is more than a hobby. It's a reclamation of knowledge that might otherwise be lost. It's not just about bringing a game back to life, but about understanding the genesis of gaming itself.

Every line of code is part of a larger story. By unlocking these relics, we gain more than just a functional server. We gain insight into our own evolution as builders of digital worlds.

The next time you see someone working on an old server, don't dismiss it as a trip down memory lane. See it as a testament to the engineering spirit. A reminder that today's cutting-edge is tomorrow's history, and worth understanding.

In a world that values the new, let's not forget the power of old code. Sometimes, the forgotten lessons are the ones most worth learning.

Maybe it's time we start treating old codebases less like obsolete relics, and more like the digital artifacts they are.

Bun's Shift to Rust: Why Ecosystems Matter More Than Code

I read about Bun moving from Zig to Rust and it made me think about the real drivers behind choosing a programming language. It's not always about which language is technically better. It often comes down to ecosystem and community support.

Bun wanted a language that scales with its ambition. Zig is fast and lean, but Rust brings a bustling community and mature tooling. That's not something you can ignore when planning for the long haul.

Developers like to think they're choosing the best tool for the job. We're all just gambling on ecosystems. The biggest language might not be the best technically, but the community and libraries are hard currency in long-term project viability.

Remember when Node.js took off? It wasn't because JavaScript was the best. It was because npm made it easy to plug in whatever library you needed. Community is what propelled Node to the top. Not the elegance of the language.

I’ve been in those rooms where someone pitches a language purely on elegance or speed. But I’ve also been the guy who had to debug a niche language at 3am with no Stack Overflow help in sight. I'd take a less elegant language that solves my problems right any day.

With Bun, the choice to move to Rust probably wasn't just about what happens under the hood. It was about making a bet on a language with a robust ecosystem. One that can support its ambitions without reinventing wheels for every little thing.

Developer experience matters. Sure, Zig is exciting, but how many developers are comfortable with it? Rust is gaining ground in the industry precisely because it balances performance with safety and has widespread community adoption.

The decision developers face is rarely about language specs in isolation. It's about how quickly you can get up and running, how much support you can find, and how far the language can take you before you hit the tarmac and need to file down your own landing gear.

I've seen systems kneecapped by their reliance on a language that couldn't keep up with new requirements. Libraries go stale, maintainers move on, and suddenly your stack feels like a museum exhibit.

Rust's ecosystem means it's not just a language. It's a full suite of options backed by forums, tooling, and documentation that's good enough to get you out of a jam at 2am. That's the kind of safety net Bun is betting on.

There's a lesson here beyond Bun and Rust. When you're making technology choices, you're betting on the community just as much as the code. Are there enough people who care? Are there reliable libraries? How fast can you onboard someone new?

Choosing the right ecosystem is crucial. You want good infrastructure, decent support, and a community that helps when you're in a pinch.

The mistake is thinking you're choosing a language when you're actually buying into a whole community. A language with a weak ecosystem can turn into a ghost town, with your project as the only resident.

Rust isn't perfect. Its learning curve is notoriously steep. But the momentum behind it is undeniable. It's why companies like Dropbox have moved critical components over to it. They're not just buying performance; they're buying into the future of the language.

I've seen firsthand what happens when you roll the dice on a language without considering the community. It's not just about whether you'll be able to use it in a decade. It's about the little things. The libraries that save you weeks of work, the documentation that prevents weeks of cursing under your breath.

So when I see Bun shift from Zig to Rust, I get it. They're not just rewriting code. They're reshaping their future by anchoring it on a more solid ecosystem.

The next time you're tempted by a slick new language, consider: is this the right neighbor? Or will you find yourself the last person on the block, wishing you'd picked the neighborhood with the better support?

The language you choose is less of a technical decision and more of an evolutionary one. You're picking where your project gets its nutrients from. Choose wisely.

Because in the end, it's the ecosystem that makes or breaks your codebase, not the syntax.

Your Abstraction Layers Are Costing You in Ways You Didn't Consider

I stumbled upon a discussion about the hidden costs of software abstractions and immediately thought of the last time our team spent weeks untangling a performance issue buried under layers of 'helpful' abstractions.

We had introduced an abstraction layer to simplify database calls. It was supposed to save time by letting developers handle data without diving into SQL.

Instead, our P99 latency stretched beyond a second on key operations. Not a minor hiccup, but a full-blown issue.

Each layer added its own overhead. Abstraction layers often look cleaner and are easier to understand when skimming code. But every layer is a double-edged sword.

Each abstraction can hide inefficiencies and make debugging a nightmare. You don't see everything going on under the hood until it's too late.

In our case, we had multiple serialization steps and hidden network calls nestled within the layers. The pretty interface masked a brutal reality.

And then comes the technical debt. These 'clean' abstractions become deeply embedded. Refactoring them out isn't just tough—it's daunting.

Our quick fix for developer ease became a long-term headache. One that cost us man-hours and stressed out on-call engineers.

The short-term gains are seductive. Faster shipping, less cognitive load, and a false sense of simplicity.

But simplicity up front often means complexity later. During the post-mortem, we realized we lacked a rigorous evaluation of the trade-offs before implementing that abstraction.

We used a popular ORM for our database interactions. Great for quick development, but it was generating suboptimal SQL queries that resulted in a 3x performance hit. It wasn't just one query. It was a pattern across the board.

We now try to ask ourselves: Does this abstraction genuinely save time or just shift the burden? What performance costs are we accepting?

It's not about avoiding abstractions. We still need them to manage complexity. But we need to wield them with caution.

Understanding what an abstraction is hiding is as important as its visible benefits.

One approach we've adopted is to periodically review our abstractions. Are they still serving us, or are we serving them?

We've also started maintaining clearer documentation about what's hidden behind each layer. Knowledge needs to be shared, not shrouded.

Some might say this is overkill. But when you lose a week to debugging spaghetti code masked as abstraction, you start valuing clarity.

Another dimension to consider is how closely tied your abstractions are to your infrastructure. We had a system tightly coupled to a cloud provider's specific service. When that service API changed, it broke our abstraction, and we were left scrambling to patch it.

Many performance bottlenecks aren't due to the algorithm itself but due to how it's wrapped.

Your abstraction layer could be making unnecessary HTTP requests, leading to an unexpected amount of latency. We learned this the hard way when our service was hit with 100ms delays because of redundant calls added by our abstraction layer.

Next time you're about to introduce a shiny new abstraction, ask the hard questions. Challenge its necessity as much as its elegance.

The abstraction layers you add today might just become the bottlenecks that haunt you tomorrow.

Choose your abstractions like you're picking a long-term partner, not a weekend fling.

Your Code Isn’t Yours: The Silent Shift in Authorship with AI Tools

It was a regular Tuesday when I stumbled into a heated debate about VS Code's Copilot appending 'Co-Authored-by Copilot' to every commit. This wasn't a bug; it was a feature that set the engineering community buzzing.

I've been around codebases long enough to know that authorship matters. It’s not just about vanity. It’s about accountability, traceability, and understanding the history of a code change.

When a tool like Copilot silently puts its digital fingerprints on your commits, it raises questions. Does the tool deserve credit for a suggestive correction or a block of code it generated? This affects our perception of code ownership.

Code authorship helps track who did what, when, and why. It's crucial during code reviews, debugging sessions, and post-mortems.

Imagine you're dissecting a runtime issue. You pull up the commit history only to find half your repo co-authored by an AI. It turns transparency into a fog of ambiguity.

The ethical implications are even murkier. Is it right for an AI to claim co-authorship for a code suggestion that wasn’t much more than autocomplete on steroids?

Some argue that crediting Copilot is about transparency—acknowledging the tool’s role in the development process. But there’s a fine line between transparency and needless clutter.

This feels like diluting authorship. It’s akin to giving credit to the IDE for flagging a syntax error or the compiler for catching a type mismatch.

The real kicker here is the silent nature of these changes. Developers often weren’t even aware their commits were getting this extra tag until they saw their logs.

This lack of awareness can undermine trust in the tools we use daily. Trust is foundational in engineering cultures, especially as teams grow and rely increasingly on automation.

AI-assisted tools are game-changers. They crank up productivity, catch errors sooner, and often make us look smarter by suggesting things we didn’t even know we needed.

But when these tools silently alter how we attribute work, they cross a line that many developers didn't even know existed until it was breached.

One potential solution could be more explicit user consent. Before appending its signature, Copilot could prompt users, giving them the choice to acknowledge the contribution.

This might slow down the workflow slightly, but it reinforces that trust and lets developers decide the tool's level of involvement in their work.

Another approach could be context-sensitive authorship attribution. If Copilot actually generates a significant function or solves a problem, maybe then it deserves a nod.

Yet, these solutions only address symptoms, not the root of the issue.

The crux of the matter is redefining what authorship means in the AI era. Should every tool that contributes to our process be tagged as a co-author?

It’s a slippery slope. Next thing you know, your favorite linter will be demanding recognition for each line of tidied-up code.

Ultimately, the transparency vs. authorship debate isn’t just about lines of code. It's about the integrity of the development process and respecting the craft.

As AI tools continue to evolve, developers must stay vigilant. We need to ensure tools amplify our work, not obscure the contributions of individuals.

Before you let Copilot or any AI take credit, consider if this tool truly deserves a seat at the authorship table.

The challenge isn't in using AI to generate code. It's in maintaining clarity about who’s responsible for what when things go wrong.

Maintaining that clarity is more critical now than ever.

Why Changing a Fan's Color Isn't Just Cosmetic

I read Noctua's blog post about their process for releasing black fan versions. It made me rethink how we often overlook the complexities in seemingly simple product variations. I assumed releasing black versions of their fans was about aesthetics. It's far more intricate than that.

Noctua fans are known for their beige and brown colors. When they announced black versions, the internet buzzed. Changing the plastic color seems straightforward. It's not.

Noctua uses a specific plastic blend for durability and performance. Altering the color means adjusting this formula. This isn't just cosmetic. It affects acoustic properties and thermal conductivity.

These changes impact fan speed, noise levels, and vibration frequencies. A black fan must perform like a Noctua fan. It's where aesthetics meet engineering.

The tooling is another challenge. The molds for fan blades and frames are precision instruments. Reworking them for different materials or colors involves testing and recalibration. A small deviation can affect airflow efficiency.

In tech, we love the mantra 'Move fast and break things.' But every change can ripple through a manufacturing process as intricate as this. Noctua crafts products, not just releases them.

Supply chains add another layer. Black plastic isn't just paint. It's a different material source, possibly from a different supplier. Each change means new logistics and contracts while maintaining quality control.

This might sound convoluted. But think of your own work. Debugging a production issue often traces back to a minor configuration change. Product design faces similar chaos.

Manufacturing is precise, and color can disrupt if not handled correctly. Every detail, even inconsequential ones, can cause a cascade of adjustments.

We often see aesthetics as simple, a choice added at the end. In reality, it's intertwined with function. Making a fan black isn't just about looking cool; it must work in a new form.

I've seen this in software too. A 'simple' UI tweak can mean refactoring CSS, adjusting JavaScript, and dealing with side effects. Users see a new button; developers see the iceberg tip.

Noctua's black fans tell a story of patience. They refuse to compromise on quality for speed. It's a lesson in valuing craftsmanship and understanding the depth behind what seems obvious.

Every time someone suggests a 'small change' in a project, I think of this. What looks simple often hides layers of complexity. Whether it's a black fan or a software feature, the details matter.

Next time you see a product variation hit the shelves, remember Noctua's journey. It wasn't just about changing a color. It was about maintaining their reputation without skipping any beats.

In engineering, whether software or hardware, looks can be deceivingly complex. Next time you're about to say, 'Just make it black,' remember—it's never just that simple.

Your Linux Kernel Security Is Always a Step Behind

Last week, a major Linux kernel vulnerability was disclosed without any prior warning. Sysadmins were left scrambling. This isn't a one-off event; it's a recurring issue in how we handle vulnerabilities.

Open-source projects value transparency, but kernel vulnerabilities seem to operate differently. The argument for secrecy is to prevent attackers from exploiting the vulnerability first. However, this leaves sysadmins and distro maintainers in a constant race to catch up.

Waking up to a CVE that affects your core infrastructure, announced publicly with no warning, tests your response strategy. It's like starting a race with your laces untied.

Security is complex. Sharing information too soon can tip off bad actors. Yet, in open-source, our community is supposed to be our strength. Without advance notice, we're effectively working with one hand tied behind our back.

The lack of early warnings isn't just inconvenient; it's a risk multiplier. In enterprise environments with hundreds or thousands of instances, the gap between announcement and patch can have serious implications. During those critical hours or days, systems remain vulnerable.

I've been on the receiving end of those emergency patches. Once they're out, everyone scrambles: packaging the update, testing it under pressure, deploying it system-wide. It's chaos on a tightrope.

The kernel maintainers do a fantastic job, but the current security disclosure system leaves distribution maintainers in the dark. This isn't just about the kernel team—it's about the broader ecosystem, which thrives on collaboration.

Some distros have built their own networks to share early warnings. That's a temporary fix, not a solution. We need a formalized early warning system that respects both the need for secrecy and the community's need for preparedness.

Consider how the private sector manages disclosures. Companies like Microsoft have private channels to inform key partners ahead of public disclosure. Open-source could adopt a similar model—not to exclude, but to include the right people at the right time.

This isn't just about admins and maintainers. It's about end-users who depend on us to keep their systems secure. It's about preventing the next big outage, the next data breach, the next zero-day exploit before it happens.

Coordinating an early disclosure system is a logistical challenge. But if open-source wants to be a viable alternative for mission-critical applications, we need to step up our security game.

Open-source is built on trust. Trust that the community will act in its best interests, that contributors will take security as seriously as any proprietary vendor. Without advance notifications, that trust wears thin.

We need to have the hard conversation. We need to find the balance between transparency and security. Because right now, the only ones getting a head start are the attackers.

The Linux kernel is many things—a technical marvel, a community triumph. But its security disclosure process? That's its Achilles' heel.

Until we fix it, every new vulnerability is a ticking time bomb. And we're all just waiting for it to go off.

Why Your NoSQL Freedom Might Just Be a Trap

Last week, I was knee-deep in a project post-mortem where the allure of MongoDB's schema-less design had morphed into a nightmare. Initial excitement about flexibility turned into a mess of inconsistent data models that nobody wanted to touch.

Teams often embrace document-oriented NoSQL databases for their perceived freedom. No constraints, no tables, no columns to rigidly define. Just toss in your JSON documents and call it a day. But that freedom often breeds chaos without discipline.

In this project, multiple services wrote to the same MongoDB collection. Each service had its own interpretation of a “customer” document. One decided that phone numbers should be an array. Another stuck to a simple string. A third didn’t include it at all.

When a simple query needed a customer phone number, it turned into a guessing game. Do we check if it’s an array first? Do we assume it’s always a string? What happens when it’s missing?

That's the trap. Document-oriented databases give you flexibility but at the cost of structure. Structure is what keeps your sanity when systems grow complex.

Contrast this with a relational database. You’ve got schemas. You’ve got constraints. It forces you to think about your data model upfront. Not exciting, but it stops you from coloring outside the lines later.

I've seen teams spend months cleaning up inconsistent data models in MongoDB that could have been avoided with a few hours of upfront design. Racing to ship features often means schema design gets neglected, but neglect this and you pay back with interest.

The flexibility of a document-oriented database can be a lifesaver, especially when you need to iterate quickly or when your data model is uncertain. I’ve been in startups where pivoting the core data model overnight was what kept us afloat.

Even then, establishing some form of internal guidelines was essential. JSON schema validation or creating a versioned document structure can provide consistency without sacrificing flexibility.

Another hidden cost in the flexibility is the tooling. Most developers have spent years with SQL, and the ecosystem of tools and best practices around relational databases is mature. When you go NoSQL, you’re choosing a whole new set of tools, most of which are still playing catch-up.

Joining collections in MongoDB like in a SQL database is painful. Aggregation pipelines are powerful but come with a learning curve that can be a stumbling block for engineers used to simpler SQL joins.

Last year, I had to explain to a team why a simple aggregation query was eating more resources than they budgeted. They'd treated it like a SQL join, resulting in unexpected memory spikes and occasional crashes.

The solution isn’t about rejecting NoSQL or embracing SQL blindly. It’s about understanding the trade-offs. If you’re going NoSQL, enforce some structure. Create guidelines. Use validation tools. Monitor for data model sprawl.

Recognize that the freedom NoSQL provides is a double-edged sword. It can empower you or strangle you with its own rope.

Pick your database like you pick your battles. Know why you choose each, and be prepared for the consequences that come with that choice.

The real challenge isn't just which database to use, but whether you can handle the freedom—or chains—that come with it.

More RAM Won't Save You from Bad Decisions

The article 'Mo RAM, Mo Problems' hit home. I've seen teams blindly add more RAM to solve performance issues, only to create new ones. It's a classic case of treating symptoms while ignoring the root cause.

More RAM is a quick fix for inefficiencies. Teams double their memory and still find performance issues. The app was inefficiently using resources.

Adding RAM promises instant relief. No code changes, just more space. But when the quick fix fails, you're left with bigger problems.

Consider garbage collection. More RAM means a larger heap, leading to longer GC pauses. I've added RAM only to find latency spikes haunting us consistently.

Huge memory profiles can mask memory leaks. I've watched services consume gigabytes of RAM, only to find a forgotten cache expanding unchecked. Adding more RAM just feeds the problem.

More RAM allows for aggressive caching. Sounds great until cache invalidation becomes a nightmare. Stale data appears at the worst moments.

In dev environments, adding RAM seems like a fix for sluggishness. But when code hits production, it crumbles under real-world data. Optimized for overkill, not reality.

Memory mismanagement can break fault tolerance. More RAM gives false security. A node fails, but redundancy plans falter because resilience was never tested under realistic load.

Operational costs rise too. More RAM means more power, more heat, and complex cooling. A simple fix becomes an operational headache.

Here's a story. We had a service handling heavy image processing. The team doubled the RAM. Instant relief, then chaos. A third-party library hiccuped with ample resources.

Problems multiplied. The library bug wasn't an issue when RAM was tight. More RAM delayed the inevitable reckoning.

Chasing RAM can become a rabbit hole. Each new gigabyte creates an illusion of stability. But it's a tower built on sand. Performance problems resurface like a bad sequel.

I'm not saying never add more RAM. Sometimes, it's right. But it should be the last resort, not the first.

Evaluate your architecture. Look at your code. Profile your service. Find real bottlenecks before throwing hardware at the problem.

Optimize first. Refactor that greedy algorithm. Rethink that joins-heavy query. Then see if you still need more RAM.

Every tool in your stack has limits. Know yours. More RAM can trick you into thinking you've surpassed them when you're just running faster towards them.

In the end, balance is key. Performance isn't just about resources; it's about judicious use of them. Adding RAM isn't a panacea. It's a crutch. Walk without it whenever you can.

Next time you're tempted to bump up your RAM, consider if you're solving the problem or just hiding it.

If you can't confidently answer that, dig in and find out what's really eating your performance.

More RAM won't save you from bad decisions. But good decisions will save you from needing more RAM.

Concurrence, Neural Nets, and the Evolution of Expertise

Go’s concurrency model isn’t magic. Everyone nods at goroutines and channels as if they’ve solved all multi-threading woes, but my experiences scream otherwise. Traditional thread-based programming taught us to wrestle with locks, race conditions, and carefully structured state. In Go, you trade that explicit control for a model that invites a certain complacency. When a system overflows with goroutines doing nothing but channel-passing, the problem suddenly isn’t so much execution as it is design. I’ve seen teams deploy services where channel feeds were assumed to be an infinite resource, only to later watch them crumble under back-pressure and silent dead goroutines. The abstraction of concurrency is seductive until you hit the concrete case of resource leakage when channels aren’t properly buffered or closed.

Deadlock and livelock aren’t just textbook footnotes—they’re realities that tear systems apart, often at inopportune moments. While the conventional view treats deadlocks as the obvious outcome of poor lock ordering, and livelocks as merely systems where nothing gets accomplished due to overactive conflict, the true picture is bleaker. I’ve seen systems where two components endlessly signal readiness without ever obtaining a mutually satisfactory lock. The problem isn’t “one or the other” but a narrow architectural oversight that fails to consider fallbacks or timeout approaches. Real systems need mechanisms that both detect stalling conditions and allow for a safe exit path without sacrificing state integrity. When settling on lock design, you have to contemplate that a common cause for what appears as a livelock may be external and not a mishandled mutex at all; it might be an external constraint that means the system is fighting on too many fronts.

The debate between torch.nn.Module and torch.nn.functional in PyTorch is yet another instance where the high-level abstraction looks attractive until you consider sensible production needs. In practice, it’s not just about the trade-off between encapsulation and flexibility. I learned that Module is less about organizing code and more about controlling state. When you rely on the functional API, you’re spending more cycles tracking tensor dimensions and manually wiring parameters, which can be fatal when you’re debugging model scalabilities or deploying to production. I remember debugging a custom network where the functional call subtly overridden an expected module’s behavior, leading to weight sharing issues that echoed through a distributed training regime. It forced me to sharpen my model definitions and lean on Module to manage not just state but to give a structural integrity that functional can’t supply out-of-the-box. It’s a choice that can impact the performance of your code in subtle ways—memory usage, gradient flow, and even parallelization suffer if you don’t enforce the Module structure when needed.
