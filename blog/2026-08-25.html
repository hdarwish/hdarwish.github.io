Local inference isn't local when a server issues the identity

Your laptop can generate the picture without the cloud doing any of the math. It still won't hand you the file without the cloud's permission slip.

The local part is entirely real, which is what makes this worth staring at. Xusheng Li pulled four .onnxe files out of the Paint app directory, XORed them against a key registry in segapi.dll, and got valid ONNX graphs back — mager.onnx alone is 15,284 nodes. Actual weights, actual Stable Diffusion, executing on the NPU in your machine, sitting on your disk in C:\Program Files\WindowsApps\. Nobody is faking the inference.

And then Paint declines to give you the image unless a remote server has first handed back a GUID to stamp invisibly into the pixels. If that stamping step fails, the successfully generated bitmap is thrown away and you get an error.

One failure path tells you more about how a feature was designed than any privacy disclosure ever will.

You almost certainly evaluate "local" as a privacy property: does my data leave the machine? That's the question everyone asks, and it's the wrong shape. The question that actually predicts behavior is about dependency — what must be reachable for the operation to complete — and those two questions come apart in precisely the way this feature demonstrates.

Walk the call tree. CocreatorViewModel::GenerateImageAsync calls into StableDiffusionHelpers::GenerateAsync, and look at the argument list: it already takes a watermarkId. The identity exists before a single tensor has been multiplied. It came from the prompt moderation round-trip — the server returns two things, the moderated prompt and a GUID. Sixteen bytes of server-issued identity, riding shotgun into a local inference call.

Generation happens on your silicon. Microsoft.ImageCreation.ImageGenerator produces the result, output safety checks run, and then Paint::AI::AddWatermark(bitmap, watermarkId) hands the pixels and the GUID to Watermarker.dll!WmkWriteWatermark. Success gives you a watermarked bitmap and a StableDiffusionResult. Failure turns a completed generation into an error.

The encoder is charmingly, revealingly picky. Payload under 16 bytes returns -6; over 16 bytes returns -5 — two distinct error codes for the same wrongness, which I enjoy more than I should — and then the copy loop ignores the length parameter entirely and hard-codes i < 16. The GUID isn't embedded raw, either. The wrapper builds an 18-byte, 144-bit message: 0x4c, the sixteen GUID bytes, then a checksum that is simply the sum of those bytes mod 256. That checksum is the tell — you only add one when something downstream intends to read the message back and needs to know it read it correctly.

The core encoder rounds usable image dimensions down to multiples of eight, keeps 144 counters, one per bit, and requires every bit to find a home.

Every bit. No partial credit. Which means a small or awkwardly-shaped canvas can simply lack the room to carry 144 bits of message — and a canvas that can't carry the message is a canvas you don't get to keep.

Now notice what this path is not wired to. Paint has a visible-watermark setting — Never, AskEveryTime, Always — which drives a completely separate route through GetPerceptibleWatermarkSvg and AddPerceptibleWatermark to composite a little Copilot logo into the corner. That's the one with a toggle. The GUID path goes through AddWatermark, has no setting anywhere, and is load-bearing.

An analogy, and I'll tell you where it breaks. Picture a print shop that owns its own press: all the machinery is on-site, nothing gets faxed to headquarters, the ink is yours. But every sheet must carry a serial number, the serial numbers arrive from head office one at a time, and the press physically will not release a sheet it couldn't stamp. The press is local. The press run is not. Where the analogy breaks: a serial number on paper announces itself as a serial number, whereas this one lives in the pixel statistics, spread across 144 counters, invisible in whatever you post next.

So forget the watermark for a moment and look only at the control flow. A successful NPU generation — weights loaded from your disk, tensors computed on your hardware, no cloud round-trip for the expensive part — gets discarded because a stamping step couldn't complete using a token that a server minted. That isn't a privacy architecture. It isn't even primarily a moderation architecture. It is a system that has concluded the artifact is invalid without an externally-issued identity, and has said so in the most emphatic way software can say anything: by failing.

Local inference isn't local when a server issues the identity.

Sit with that. It generalizes a long way past Paint.

The portable idea: read the failure paths, not the feature list. Marketing describes the happy path, and the happy path only tells you what a system wants to be. The error branches tell you what it actually depends on. A component whose absence degrades the result is optional. A component whose absence destroys the result is the spine. In Paint, watermarking is the spine and the NPU is a performance optimization.

Which sharpens the whole on-device pitch into two claims that sound alike and aren't. "Runs locally" is a statement about where the FLOPs happen. "Works offline" is a statement about the dependency graph. A vendor can say the first with a completely straight face while quietly declining to say the second — and provenance machinery, C2PA metadata, restricted save formats, invisible payloads, is exactly the kind of thing that lives in the second graph and never shows up in the first.

None of which makes provenance a bad goal. Being able to tell what a machine made is going to matter enormously, possibly more than most privacy fights of the last decade. But provenance-first architecture means the artifact belongs to the chain of custody before it belongs to you, and that ordering is not a policy paragraph you can argue with. It's an if statement.

Your NPU did the work. The server signed the receipt. Guess which one the code treats as mandatory.
