AI's Economic Bubble: The Margin Collapse is Inevitable

I stumbled upon a post about AI margin collapse and it felt like déjà vu. It reminded me of the dot-com bubble. We're heading for a similar shakeout.

Martin Alderson's post on the potential collapse of AI margins with the advent of GLM 5.2 raised a critical point. The AI market is on the cusp of an economic upheaval. As technology saturates, the real challenge for AI companies isn't just about advancement but survival amidst shrinking margins.

The big misconception is that the cost of AI is all about the initial training of models. In reality, while the training involves significant upfront investment, it's the inference stage where costs scale with demand. Every time you use an AI, it incurs a cost. I've seen inference costs creeping up to $0.01 per API call in some setups. Multiply that by millions of calls—this isn't pocket change.

Historically, AI companies have enjoyed high margins. Take OpenAI, for example. They charge a hefty rate for API use, often maintaining around a 60-90% gross margin. But as more models like GLM 5.2 emerge, with open weights and easier migration paths, the competitive landscape shifts.

GLM 5.2 from Z.ai, for instance, is pushing the boundaries. It's a legitimate competitor to current powerhouses like Opus and GPT. This isn't a far-fetched vision of the future; it's happening now. The model offers a near-identical performance to its rivals for many tasks, albeit slower in interactive use due to its 'thinking' time.

The saturation is evident. Companies can easily switch to open models with minimal friction. Z.ai and Fireworks provide drop-in replacements, making migration as simple as redirecting an endpoint. This isn't the cumbersome lock-in that giants like Microsoft would have you endure.

With low switching costs, AI companies can't rely on customer inertia. The margins, which look appealing today, might not hold as competitors offer similar capabilities at lower prices. This is particularly challenging for newer entrants and smaller players who might not withstand prolonged price wars.

Consider the vital role that support services and ecosystem development play. AI isn't just about the core technology. Companies provide APIs, integration support, and other services. These offerings currently bulk up revenue streams, but as tech commoditizes, they'll need to find new ways to add value.

The real threat is from open-source models that make AI broadly accessible. This democratization of AI capabilities dilutes the premium that existing providers charge. Web search capabilities and data privacy concerns, pointed out by Alderson, emphasize the need for robust auxiliary services to differentiate offerings.

I've witnessed firsthand what happens when margins collapse. In a previous role, we relied heavily on a proprietary NLP model. When cheaper, open-source alternatives emerged, our usage costs plummeted, but so did our competitive edge. We found ourselves scrambling to integrate additional features and provide superior customer support just to keep our heads above water.

The market's eventual saturation is inevitable. AI companies need to adapt, refocusing on efficiency and new value propositions. Those who fail may find themselves unable to sustain operations, echoing stories from the early 2000s dot-com crash.

I foresee partnerships and consolidation trends as companies seek strength in numbers. It's a game of survival, and only the adaptable will thrive.

For the engineers and decision-makers reading this, the lesson is clear: consider your costs carefully and think about long-term sustainability. As margins dwindle, understanding the finer economics of AI isn't just for the finance team—it's everyone's business.

Whether you're building, maintaining, or using AI, the shakeout will impact you. Prepare now, while you can still shape your destiny rather than react.

The current AI boom disguises an upcoming financial reckoning. Recognizing it early offers a chance to steer through, rather than sink.
