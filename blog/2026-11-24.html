The browser monopoly is a cost decision, not a physics law

Ladybird just hit alpha with a new engine built from scratch. The real news isn't that it works—it's that it didn't require a billion dollars to get there.

browsersopen-sourceengineeringmonopoly

The difficulty of building a web engine is often conflated with the cost of building one. These are different variables, and confusing them has protected incumbents from competition for two decades.

The distinctionTechnical necessity vs. engineering scale

01

The Lie of the Multi-Year Effort

For twenty years, the standard argument against new browsers was simple: it’s too hard. You need thousands of engineers. You need a decade. You need a Fortune 500 balance sheet. It was framed as a barrier to entry so high that only the "Big Three" could clear it.

Ladybird, now in alpha for macOS and Linux, just disproved that framing. Andreas Kling and a small team built an engine "from the ground up, and not a fork." It is modest. It is functional. It crashes sometimes. But it exists.

The engine is new. The browser is functional. That is the point. The barrier was not the physics of rendering HTML; it was the assumption that you needed a massive organization to do it.

02

Alpha as a Signal, Not a Product

An alpha browser is not a product you buy; it is a signal you decode. Ladybird is a rolling channel, pushing updates regularly. It supports WebDriver and the Firefox DevTools protocol. It invites you to break it.

The source code is open. The crash reports are readable. The privacy policy is explicit about what happens when you send them. This is not a product launch; it is an invitation to participate in the engineering process.

The "Big Three" do not operate this way. They do not invite you to read their crash reports. They do not push out rolling updates that break your workflow for the sake of transparency. Ladybird’s alpha is a different kind of artifact: it is proof of concept, not proof of market dominance.

03

Why Scale Was the Moat

If building a browser were purely a technical challenge, we would have seen more new engines. We haven’t, because the cost of *maintaining* a browser is high. The cost of *building* one was always lower than we were told.

The incumbents benefited from a feedback loop: their size made them seem necessary, and their necessity justified their size. Ladybird breaks the loop. It shows that the technical core—the engine, the rendering, the network stack—can be built by a small team.

The metric that matters here is not market share; it is the *rate of iteration*. Ladybird improves quickly. Until now, you had to build it from source to see that. Now you can download it. The friction has dropped.

04

The Engineering Choice

The monopoly is not a technical inevitability. It is an engineering choice, made by organizations that preferred scale over speed. Ladybird is an engineering choice, made by a team that preferred speed over scale.

This does not mean Ladybird will replace Chrome or Firefox. It will not. But it means the gate is open. The next browser engine can be built by a small team. The next one after that can be built by a small team.

The real story is not that a browser exists. It is that the excuse for why no new browsers exist is gone.

The turn

We were told the barrier was technical. It was actually financial and organizational. Ladybird didn’t build a better browser; it built a smaller one. And that smaller one is the threat.

Analogy Building a browser is like building a car. The physics of combustion is known. The difficulty is not in the engine design; it’s in the supply chain, the testing, and the regulatory compliance. Ladybird built the engine. The incumbents built the supply chain.

Where it breaks Browsers are not cars. They are ecosystems, not just machines. The network effects of a browser are far stronger than the brand loyalty of a car.
