The Glue Is People, Not Models

Complex systems don't fail because the code is bad. They fail because the humans who know how to break them are being replaced by models that cannot care about the break.

complex-systemsai-ethicsinfrastructure

The stability of critical infrastructure is a function of human attention, not algorithmic output.

The distinctionThe difference between a system that is technically flawed but operationally safe, and one that is technically flawed and operationally fragile, is the presence of smart people who give a shit.

01 / 04

The ACH Paradox

The Automated Clearing House (ACH) network is, by any objective technical metric, a disaster. It is an ancient architecture, riddled with inherent flaws, and encumbered with decades of legacy baggage that would make a modern software architect weep. It is slow, it is brittle, and it is fundamentally fragile. Yet, operationally, it is one of the most reliable systems in the United States. Money moves every single day, and it almost never breaks. This stark mismatch between the technical reality of the code and the operational success of the system is not a bug in the logic; it is a feature of the people. Justin Michael, writing for VioletPixel, identifies the single factor that resolves this paradox: smart people who give a shit. These are not just engineers; they are individuals who possess two specific, compounding traits. First, they have intimate, almost visceral knowledge of the system's internal mechanics. Second, they have the drive and determination to force the system to work, even when it wants to stop. Every edge case, every routing error, every legacy quirk is handled by a human who knows exactly where the wire is frayed and has the will to tape it shut. The system works not because the code is robust, but because the humans are stubborn.

This relationship is not static; it is a positive feedback loop that defines the health of critical infrastructure. You have to care about a complex system enough to dive deep into its internals. But once you know how it works, you care more. This is especially true when the system is critical infrastructure. The knowledge deepens the commitment, and the commitment deepens the knowledge. It is a flywheel that only spins if a human is physically or intellectually present in the machine. Generative AI does not participate in this loop. It has no stake in the outcome. It does not feel the weight of a failed transaction. It does not lose sleep over a potential outage. It is a tool, a very sophisticated one, but it is not a participant. It provides output, but it does not provide the *attention* that makes complex systems resilient. The ACH network survives because humans are in the loop, not because the loop is automated.

02 / 04

The Illusion of Competence

The danger of LLMs in this context is not that they are stupid. It is that they are *good enough* to fool management. To a leader who is not in the trenches, the output of an LLM looks like the output of a senior engineer. Code that runs, text that reads well, solutions that appear logical. But underneath, the mechanism is different. LLMs are pattern matchers. They predict the next token based on statistical probability. They do not understand the system. They do not notice when things feel wrong. They do not connect dots that aren't explicitly present in the training data. What looks like intelligence is an illusion propped up by a massive amount of data strip-mined from the commons with weights that nudge the output toward a generally desirable shape. It is a simulation of competence, not competence itself. The math and logic that power LLMs are as cold, unfeeling, and indifferent as the laws of physics.

Smart people who give a shit notice things. They feel the vibration of a system that is about to go off the rails. They know how to handle the unique, the unprecedented, the edge case that has never happened before. LLMs fall short here because they are indifferent. Their logic is unfeeling. They are designed to tell you what you want to hear, not what is true. When a system starts to fail, an LLM will likely generate a plausible-sounding explanation that misses the actual root cause. A human who cares will notice the anomaly, the slight deviation from the norm, and act on it. This is the critical difference. The LLM is a mirror, reflecting the user's expectations back at them. The human is a sensor, detecting the reality of the system. In critical infrastructure, the sensor is more valuable than the mirror.

03 / 04

The Erosion of Institutional Memory

The real tragedy is not the replacement of humans with AI. It is the hollowing out of the humans who remain. When you tell an engineer to stop doing the work and start babysitting an LLM, you are robbing them of the struggle that makes them smart. You are severing their connection to the system. Every prompt they write moves them one step further away from the internal mechanics. Every generated output that gets merged into the codebase means they understand less about how the system actually works. This is a slow death. The system doesn't break all at once. It just becomes a little more fragile. A little less resilient. A little more dependent on the next human who walks in. And because the humans are less connected, less smart, less caring, the system becomes more likely to fail. This is the collapse Michael warns about. It is not a single event. It is a gradual erosion of the glue.

Capitalism has never been particularly kind to smart people who give a shit, but it has begrudgingly recognized their necessity. Now, with generative AI, leadership sees an opportunity to replace them. The machine is cheaper, works longer, doesn't take sick days, and never pushes back. People who don't do the actual work—the people who aren't in the trenches—have a hard time telling the difference between a flawed system held aloft by human drive and slop provided by generative AI. To them, it's all just output. It matters because generative AI isn't smart, and it doesn't give a shit. We have moved from a world dependent on complex and fragile systems held together by smart people who give a shit to a world of complex and fragile systems run by generative AI. The people running the generative AI are not as smart, nor do they give as much of a shit. As long as the number of smart people who give a shit continues to go down, more systems will fail.

04 / 04

The Hot Stove

Michael uses the analogy of the hot stove. When you tell a child the stove is hot, some children listen. Others need to be burned to learn. Capitalism is the child that needs to be burned. Leadership has been told that LLMs can replace the smart people who give a shit. They have been told that this will increase efficiency, reduce costs, and improve output. They have not listened. They are putting their hand on the stove right now. The failures we are seeing—Microsoft 365, Air Traffic Control, United Airlines, the NSA, major banks—are not isolated incidents. They are the smoke from the burn. They are the evidence that the glue is gone. The lesson is not that AI is bad. The lesson is that you cannot replace the human element of complex system maintenance with a tool that does not care. The stove is hot. The hand is on it. The burn is coming.

We are told these failures are not related. They're not the result of a cyberattack. They're not the fault of generative AI. I guess that depends on how you look at it. But the common through line is clear: there aren't enough smart people who give a shit. The exceptions exist, which is why a lot of things are still humming along, but the trend is clear. The glue is people. The models are just the paper.

The turn

The models are not the problem. The problem is the belief that the models can do the caring.

Analogy A LLM is like a highly skilled scribe who can copy any document perfectly, but who has never seen the document, doesn't understand the context, and doesn't care if the copy is used to run a hospital or a library.

Where it breaks The analogy breaks down when the scribe is actually useful. LLMs *are* useful for many tasks. The point is not that they are useless, but that they are not a substitute for the *caring* part of the job.

If you're deciding
When
When considering AI integration into critical infrastructure maintenance
Prefer
Augmenting human experts with AI tools to handle routine tasks, freeing them to focus on edge cases and system health
Avoid
Replacing human experts with AI, or using AI to hollow out the institutional knowledge of the remaining team

The glue is people. The models are just the paper.
