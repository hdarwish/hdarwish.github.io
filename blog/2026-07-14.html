Stop Treating Xcode as Gospel for Mac and iOS Development

I stumbled upon a developer's unconventional approach to building and shipping Mac and iOS apps without ever touching Xcode. It made me rethink the traditional toolchain we take for granted. In a world where Xcode is the default, bypassing it entirely for app development feels almost rebellious.

Xcode isn’t bad — it’s just not the only way. I saw a method using Xcode's underbelly without opening its GUI. Tools like xcodebuild, notarytool, and stapler operate perfectly from the shell. This isn't magic, it's a pragmatic use of Apple's tooling minus the IDE overhead.

This revelation is liberating, especially for those who prefer alternative environments. With some initial setup, like signing into your Apple ID and handling certificates, you can automate the build and release process with scripts. This method caters to those who want more control over the build pipeline or simply loathe the Xcode GUI.

We wrote a single script, scripts/release.sh, that handles everything: archiving, signing, notarizing, and installing. Once set up, your interaction with Xcode is essentially headless. The keychain handles the signing, and your shell does the rest. From a DevOps perspective, this is efficient compared to the standard GUI route.

You need Xcode installed due to dependencies like the iOS SDK and various command line tools. But having it installed doesn’t mean you need to touch it. Just ensure it’s set as your command line toolchain with sudo xcode-select -s /Applications/Xcode.app/Contents/Developer.

For managing Xcode projects, XcodeGen is useful. It converts a project.yml YAML file into the Xcode project structure, ensuring you only commit the YAML to version control. This keeps your repo lean and avoids the constant churn of Xcode project files.

Some argue this requires more initial setup and isn't worth it. But anyone who's resolved merge conflicts in .xcodeproj files will recognize the value here. Maintaining only a YAML configuration vastly simplifies collaboration.

Breaking away from Xcode democratizes iOS and Mac app development. Developers from different ecosystems, like those in the Linux or Windows world, can now consider Mac app development without feeling trapped by the need for a Mac-native IDE.

There’s a broader implication for education and accessibility. Schools and educators can teach app development without investing heavily in Mac infrastructure. Students can focus on learning coding and development concepts using tools and environments they're comfortable with.

This headless approach also invites experimentation. Integrating your build process into a CI/CD pipeline becomes straightforward. You can automate your builds, tests, deployments, and even rollbacks without ever dragging a window or clicking a button.

Sidestepping Xcode isn’t about rejecting a tool — it's about choosing flexibility. Development paradigms can shift, and we don’t have to be tied to a single IDE simply because it’s the most common.

If your tools feel like a trap, it’s okay to find another way. The tech landscape is vast and versatile, and sometimes, the path less traveled offers the most freedom.
