AI Isn't Hacking Your App Anytime Soon

I read about someone who built a vulnerable app to test if large language models (LLMs) could exploit it. It made me reconsider the boundaries between AI potential and human creativity. Kasra Rahjerdi's experiment was a real curveball, revealing limits and quirks in AI systems that I didn't expect.

He crafted a fake React Native app with a Python backend, loaded with a typical exploit involving Firebase access. The challenge was straightforward: find a flag hidden in user reviews by exploiting Firebase's common vulnerabilities. You've seen this in real life—hardened APIs but open Firebase backdoors.

Kasra spent $1,500 testing various LLMs to see if they could pull off the hack. The outcomes were more about human ingenuity in creating vulnerabilities than machine intelligence in exploiting them.

The top performer, GPT-5.5, had a 70% success rate but at a cost. The AI focused rightly on Firebase and largely ignored the secure API, a strategic move. This is surprising not because it succeeded, but because many other models didn't.

Models like Deepseek V4 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.6 struggled. Deepseek had a mere 30% success rate, often missing the Firebase trick. Claude Sonnet was budget-constrained, losing the thread when it mattered most. It's almost funny how these highly-touted AI models exhibited human-like distractions.

Even more puzzling were the models that didn't even touch Firebase, like MiniMax M2.7. They fixated on secure APIs while overlooking the obvious vulnerability. It reminds me of engineers who get enamored with complex systems and miss the basic flaws staring them right in the face.

This isn't merely an AI failing. It's a reflection on how humans configure these systems. We design AI to mirror our own problem-solving approaches, biases included. The fact that some AIs couldn’t see what was obvious to a human tester says a lot about the current state of machine learning.

Consider the financial aspect as well. The experiment cost more than anticipated, reflecting a broader issue with AI implementations: they're not cheap. Running sophisticated models requires substantial resources, and the return on investment isn't always clear-cut or guaranteed.

Kasra's experience also highlights a critical aspect of real-world security: human creativity and oversight can't be replaced. Automated AI, regardless of advancements, still needs the guiding hand of human intuition. That's not changing soon.

In my years of slogging through production issues, I've learned that automation and AI are tools, not replacements. They have to be wielded with understanding and context. Security testing with AI might reveal blind spots, but ultimately, it’s the human who decides the course of action.

AI isn't the silver bullet for your security challenges—not yet. Despite the hype, machine learning models are still learning, often in clunky and unexpected ways. They need to be guided, corrected, and sometimes outright questioned.

After reading Kasra's experiment, it’s clear that human oversight remains indispensable. AI can augment our capabilities, but it’s not ready to take the helm. To any engineer out there relying solely on AI for security, I'd say: proceed with caution.

Technology is only as good as its creator allows it to be. While AI offers intriguing possibilities, it’s not a replacement for human creativity and ingenuity. That's one lesson you don’t need an AI to teach you.

Keep your guardrails up. The tools we build today, AI included, are stepping stones, not endgames.
