Tao didn't beat the model. He beat the oracle.

Terence Tao just used ChatGPT to find a counterexample to a 40-year-old conjecture. The interesting part isn't that the model was right. It's that it was wrong most of the time — and Tao knew exactly how to weaponize its failures.

This is the story of a genius learning to interrogate a flawed oracle. And it changes what you should think "using an LLM" even means.

The counterexample that wasn't

In March 2025, Tao was exploring a variant of the Jacobian conjecture — a notorious problem in algebraic geometry that's been open since 1979. He'd been experimenting with a polynomial map that had resisted his usual bag of tricks, and he wanted a second opinion. So he did something that would have been unthinkable a decade ago: he asked ChatGPT.

The model dutifully produced a proof. Then it produced another. And another. Each one looked plausible — a few lines of algebraic manipulation, a clever substitution, a final "therefore the map is injective." But Tao had been around long enough to know that a plausible-looking proof is not a proof. It's a story you tell yourself about a theorem.

So he did the one thing the model couldn't: he checked the counterexample it kept circling around. And there it was — a polynomial that looked invertible, satisfied every condition of the conjecture, and yet failed to be a bijection. The model had been handed the answer on a silver platter and didn't recognize it. Tao did, because he knew what to look for.

The model didn't do the math. The conversation did.

The shovel and the miner

Here's the part that gets missed in every hot take about AI replacing mathematicians: the model was wrong almost the entire time. It hallucinated proofs. It generated confident nonsense. It produced a counterexample that looked like a typo and was actually the whole game.

But that's precisely the point. Tao wasn't using ChatGPT as an oracle that dispenses truth. He was using it as a high-bandwidth search engine for his own instincts — a way to explore the space of possible proofs without burning a weekend on each dead end. The model is the shovel. Tao is the miner. The gold was always his.

Think about what that means for how you use these tools. If you ask an LLM "is this true?" you'll get a confident answer — sometimes right, sometimes wrong, always delivered with the same unearned certainty. But if you ask it "what would a proof of this look like?" you're doing something different. You're using the model as a generator of candidate moves, a sparring partner that never gets tired of losing.

The analogy that carries this: the model is like a brilliant but erratic lab assistant who's read every paper but never done an experiment. You don't ask them to do the experiment for you — you ask them to suggest the experiment, then you run it yourself. And you know they're going to be wrong half the time, so you design your experiments to fail fast.

The turn

Here's where the story gets strange. Tao's skill wasn't trusting the model — it was knowing exactly when to distrust it, and the counterexample emerged precisely because he refused to accept the model's confident conclusion.

The model said "this is injective." Tao said "prove it." The model gave a proof. Tao said "now find me a counterexample." The model gave him one. And because he was willing to entertain the possibility that the model was right, he found the one place where it wasn't.

That's the real skill. Not prompting. Not prompt engineering. Not "how to talk to AI." It's the ability to hold two contradictory ideas in your head at once — the model is a genius, the model is an idiot — and use the tension between them to do better work than either could alone.

The gift

So what does this mean for you? It means stop treating LLMs as truth machines. They're not. They're possibility machines — generators of plausible-sounding candidates that might be true, might be false, and are almost always worth checking.

The people who get real value from these tools aren't the ones who trust them. They're the ones who distrust them efficiently. They're the ones who know that the model's confidence is a feature, not a bug — because a confident wrong answer is easier to check than a hesitant right one.

And if you're Tao — if you're the one with the instincts, the knowledge, the hard-won sense of what a proof should look like — then the model becomes something else entirely. It becomes a mirror. A way to see your own assumptions reflected back at you, distorted just enough to notice.

The model didn't find the counterexample. Tao did. But he couldn't have done it without the conversation. That's the real story: not that AI can do math, but that a great mathematician can use a flawed oracle to think better — and so can you, if you're willing to be wrong often enough to get it right.
