AI didn't solve it. It made it findable.

In October 2025, Terence Tao — a Fields Medalist, one of the most careful minds in mathematics — sat down with a chatbot and, over a long conversation, produced a counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture. The conjecture had stood for forty years. The chatbot was wrong almost the entire time. And that's exactly the point.

Why a 40-year-old conjecture still matters

The Jacobian Conjecture is one of those problems that looks trivial at first. It asks: if you have a polynomial map from n-dimensional space to itself, and its Jacobian determinant (the generalized volume change) is a nonzero constant everywhere, does the map have a polynomial inverse? For a linear map, yes. For a single variable, yes. For everything else, nobody could prove it — and nobody could find a counterexample either. For four decades, the conjecture sat in the "probably true, but we can't show it" pile.

This matters beyond pure mathematics. The Jacobian Conjecture sits behind questions in algebraic geometry, robotics, and cryptography. A counterexample would reshape how we think about invertibility in polynomial systems — and it would break a few cryptographic assumptions along the way. So when Tao announced a counterexample, the math world leaned in.

What actually happened in that conversation

The full transcript is public, and it's a masterclass in what AI-assisted research looks like in practice. Tao didn't ask the chatbot to solve the conjecture. He asked it to explore. The chatbot proposed candidate counterexamples — dozens of them — and Tao, with the patience of a geologist sifting riverbed gravel, tested each one.

Most of the proposals were hallucinations. Not in the fun, creative sense — in the literal sense. The chatbot confidently presented polynomial maps that looked plausible, had the right shape, even had the right degree. But when Tao actually computed the Jacobian determinant, the constant condition failed. The inverse wasn't polynomial. The map wasn't even a counterexample.

Here's the thing about a hallucination in a mathematical context: it's not a lie. It's a guess that has the surface texture of truth. A polynomial map with a constant Jacobian looks like a very specific kind of object — and the chatbot had learned what that object looks like, without learning the deep constraint that makes it rare. It was pattern-matching on the skin without knowing the skeleton.

The search space is the real story

Think of the space of all polynomial maps as a vast, mostly empty desert. The Jacobian Conjecture claims there's a small, fertile valley where every map with constant Jacobian has a polynomial inverse — and that valley is completely enclosed. To disprove the conjecture, you need to find a single grain of sand outside the valley that still has the constant-Jacobian property.

That grain is rare. It's not just rare — it's buried under layers of almost-correct candidates that look promising but fail on the last check. The traditional approach — a human mathematician, or even a computer search — would have to grind through billions of candidates, checking each one's Jacobian determinant. That's a search problem with a needle so fine it's invisible to the naked eye.

Here's where the chatbot changes the game. It doesn't search the desert exhaustively. It generates candidates from the distribution of things that look like they might be in the valley. That's not a search — that's a probabilistic sketch of the desert's texture. The chatbot is not a metal detector. It's a divining rod that wiggles when you're near water, even if it's often wrong about exactly where the water is.

The key insight: the chatbot doesn't need to be right. It needs to be suggestive. Tao's job was to filter the suggestions — to check each one against the actual mathematical constraints, discard the hallucinations, and keep the one that had a chance. And that's exactly what he did. The winning counterexample came from a suggestion that, on its face, looked no more promising than the dozens of failures before it. But Tao tested it, and the test held.

So the AI didn't solve it. It made it findable.

The hard part wasn't getting the AI to propose a counterexample — it was knowing which of its hallucinations to take seriously and then proving it wasn't a hallucination.

That's the turn. The AI didn't do mathematics. It did something more mundane and more powerful: it expanded the search space. It turned a needle-in-a-haystack problem into a needle-in-a-smaller-haystack problem, by generating candidates that were statistically more likely to be near the target. The actual verification — the part that separates a conjecture from a theorem — was still entirely human. Tao had to compute the Jacobian, check the inverse, and write up a proof that the counterexample was genuine.

What this means for your work

If you're an engineer, you've probably felt the same pull toward AI tools. You ask a model for a code snippet, it gives you something that looks right, and you spend an hour debugging it before you realize it was a hallucination. The instinct is to throw the tool away — "it's not reliable." But Tao's approach suggests a different reframe: the tool isn't there to be reliable. It's there to be generative. Your job is to be the filter.

That's the engineering consequence: don't ask an AI for the answer. Ask it for a candidate. Then verify. The verification is the real work, and it's the part that can't be automated away, because it's the part that requires understanding what the answer is supposed to look like.

This is the same skill you already use when you read a blog post about a new library, or a Stack Overflow answer that's 90% wrong. You don't take it on faith. You run it, you test it, you see where it breaks. The AI is just a faster, more confident version of that Stack Overflow answer — and just as likely to be wrong.

Tao didn't find a counterexample because the chatbot was smart. He found it because he was smart enough to know which hallucinations to trust. The chatbot expanded the search space. The proof — the part that makes it real — was always human.

The next time an AI hands you something that looks plausible, don't ask "is this right?" Ask: "is this worth checking?" Because the search space is only getting wider — and the ones who can filter it will find the needles.
