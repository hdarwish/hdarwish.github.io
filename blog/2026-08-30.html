Your bug backlog measures your patience, not your software

Bug perception is a trained skill — installable in weeks, and quietly uninstalled by the people who use your product most.

software qualityperceptionengineering culture

Dan Luu sees hundreds to thousands of bugs a week; most people he talks to see almost none — and they are hitting the same bugs, just not perceiving them. For years he assumed the cause was that he used computers strangely. The truth turned out to be worse.

The distinctionA bug count is not a property of your software. It is a reading off the least stable instrument in your stack: human perception.

01

The people changed, not the software

When Luu started pointing bugs out to friends, the ones so inclined started seeing them too. Nothing about the software had changed — the same defects sat in the same code, firing at the same rate as before. Yet over a few weeks a person who had noticed almost nothing became someone who noticed almost everything.

The bugs did not change; the people did.

Which means bug perception is a trained skill. It can be installed in a few weeks. And, just as quietly, it can be uninstalled — most efficiently by the people who use your product every single day.

02

Attention is a budget for surprises

Perception isn't a camera that faithfully records whatever sits in front of it. It is a prediction machine, and it spends its limited attention on surprises. When a stimulus stops carrying new information — when your brain can already predict exactly what it will feel like — it quietly stops paying for the update.

Picture the glasses resting on your face, or the fridge you stop hearing until the moment it clicks off: present the entire time, and invisible the entire time, because nothing about them surprises you anymore.

Point that same machinery at a broken button and the number that moves is not the defect rate — it is your report rate. The button fails on every attempt. Your filing of it decays toward zero. The mechanism runs in silence, and silence reads, from the inside, as quality.

03

The workaround destroys the evidence

The first time the form eats your input, you notice sharply. By the tenth time you notice less. By the fiftieth your hands have grown a workaround — you copy the text before you hit submit — and you never once decided to.

The workaround fixes the symptom for you and destroys the evidence for everyone else.

It migrates the knowledge out of the part of you that can write a ticket and into the part of you that ties your shoes. The defect is still firing. It has simply stopped being something you can report, because it has stopped being something you notice.

04

Two machines, one output

Sensory adaptation is one machine: repeated exposure, attention withdrawn, the defect faded into the background of a life. Motivated fandom is a different machine altogether: ownership, loyalty, and the reflex to defend a thing rather than see it clearly. They run on completely unrelated fuel.

Yet they converge on exactly the same output: invisibility.

Both take a real, still-firing defect and remove it from the perception of the person living closest to it. Same destination, reached down two different roads.

05

Dogfooding is efficient blindness

Your engineers use the product more than any customer ever will, and they use it with the deepest possible investment in believing it is good. They have had more opportunities than any living human to grow calluses over its rough edges. Every edge they have stopped feeling is an edge they can no longer put on the backlog.

And every prioritization decision the team makes is downstream of that. The backlog, the severity labels, the confident internal chorus that the thing works well — all of it is a readout from nervous systems that have been quietly adapting to the product for years.

The practice everyone recommends for quality is also the most efficient way to go blind to it.

The turn

The advice is not wrong. Dogfood it, live in it, feel what your users feel — all good. It is simply incomplete in a dangerous direction: the very exposure that teaches you the product is the same exposure that adapts you to its pain, and the adaptation is invisible from the inside. You cannot feel yourself going numb.

Analogy Your bug count is a thermometer for your product's defects: read it and you learn how broken things are.

Where it breaks A thermometer has no opinions. Your team does, and loyalty accelerates the drift — the strongest blindness sits exactly where the strongest ownership sits.

If you're deciding
When
You're triaging a backlog on a mature product with a tenured team and the reported defect rate is falling.
Prefer
Import a fresh nervous system on purpose: someone who has never touched it, doing a real task, watched in silence.
Avoid
Reading a falling defect rate as rising quality. The sensors saturated; the heat didn't drop.

The bug you have stopped feeling is still there, still firing, still being paid for on every single use. Just not by you.
