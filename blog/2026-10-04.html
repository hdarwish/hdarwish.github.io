Your agent's failure mode is a $10,000 bill

The midnight email you never want to read

Picture this: you've set up a coding agent to handle a small side project. It's been humming along, saving you hours. Then one night, an API call loops. Not a crash, not an error — just a quiet, infinite spend. By the time your email client pings at 3am with a "budget warning," the damage is done: several hundred dollars gone, maybe several thousand. You didn't wake up to a bug report. You woke up to a bill.

An AI agent without a hard budget cap isn't just expensive — it's a system whose failure mode is invisible until the invoice arrives.

Why soft caps are a trap

Most services offer soft caps: "spend more than $X, and we'll send you a warning." That sounds reasonable. It isn't. A warning email is a message to a human who is asleep, or in a meeting, or on vacation. The agent doesn't read it. The agent, by definition, is autonomous — it keeps going until something stops it. A soft cap is a suggestion. A hard cap is a stop.

Simon Willison, writing last week about this exact problem, put it plainly: soft caps "will not cut it." He's right. When the thing doing the spending is a machine that never sleeps, the only meaningful feedback loop is one that cuts power.

The mechanism: from black box to tripwire

Think about what a budget cap actually is mechanically. It's a tripwire. You define a threshold — $50, $500, whatever hurts — and the moment the meter crosses it, the system stops accepting new work. No negotiation, no retry, no "let me just finish this one call." It returns errors. That's the whole design.

And that's the point. An agent without a cap has a failure mode that's unbounded — the cost function has no maximum. It can keep spending until the credit card declines or the account is frozen. A capped agent, by contrast, fails loudly and quickly: it returns an error, the caller sees it, and the human can step in.

This is the same logic that gave us timeouts and circuit breakers in distributed systems. A timeout doesn't make a service reliable — it makes a hang observable as a failure instead of an infinite stall. A budget cap does the same for money: it converts an invisible runaway into a clear, actionable error.

Simon's article points out that AWS finally launched spending limits in September, and Google Cloud added "Spend Caps" in July. The trend is real, but it's still opt-in for most services. That's backwards. The default should be the cap, with a checkbox to remove it — "I will be responsible for subsequent charges" — not the other way around.

The turn: your agent is a system, not a friend

Here's the uncomfortable truth hiding under all this talk of budgets and billing. We keep designing agents as if they were diligent assistants — competent, well-intentioned, maybe a little too eager. But a diligent assistant who can spend your money without limit isn't diligent. It's a loaded weapon with a smile.

Without a hard cap, an agent's failure mode is infinite spending.

That's not a cost-control issue. That's an accountability issue. A capped agent is a system you can reason about: it either completes its work or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, you know exactly why — the budget tripped. An uncapped agent is a black box with a credit card. You can't debug a black box that's draining your account.

Let that sit for a moment. The cap isn't a restriction on the agent's potential. It's a definition of failure — the one signal that tells you the thing has gone off the rails.

The gift: cap everything, then go build

So what do you do with this? Three things, right now.

  • Default to caps on every API you call. If the service doesn't offer a hard cap, find one that does. Your future self will thank you when an agent goes rogue at 2am.
  • Set the cap at the level where a surprise is annoying, not catastrophic. $100 is a nuisance. $10,000 is a life event. Choose accordingly.
  • Teach your agents to check. Make them bias toward providers with hard caps, and warn them — and you — when they're about to deploy on something uncapped.

This isn't about being paranoid. It's about being free. The whole promise of agents is that they can run while you sleep. That promise is only worth anything if you know, with certainty, that the worst thing that can happen is a notification that says "budget exceeded, service paused." Not a bill. Not a panic. Just a clean, early failure.

The cap is the difference between a tool you trust and a gamble you regret. Put it on everything. Then let the agents run.

And when that midnight email finally comes — the one that says "your service was paused" instead of "you owe $10,000" — you'll know you built the right kind of system.
