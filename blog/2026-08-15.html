Cheap exploits, not good arguments, kept your encryption legal

A working cryptographer just published a worry that no cryptographer is supposed to have. Matthew Green, coming off a few days at Usenix Security in his hometown of Baltimore, sat down and wrote that he's afraid AI is going to make software much too secure.

Not "insufficiently secure." Too secure. And he means it as a threat to privacy.

If that sentence makes no sense to you, good — it shouldn't, yet. It only makes sense once you accept an unflattering thing about the last fifteen years: the reason your phone still ships with real end-to-end encryption is not that the good guys won the argument. It's that the argument was never resolved, and the thing keeping it unresolved is quietly going away.

Green's short history is worth walking through, because the shape of it is the whole point. He uses The Wire as his fossil record: season one, 2002, cops on a pole camera and a wiretap, chasing dealers with payphones and burners. Nothing in that scenario would have confused a detective airlifted in from 1989. Wiretapping was a solved administrative problem — you got the order, the carrier complied, the audio arrived. Then smartphones happened, and something genuinely new appeared: a device that didn't just carry your communications, it stored your life. For a few glorious years that was the single richest investigative resource in human history. In 2010 Apple began encrypting iPhone storage with a key derived from the user's passcode, Android followed, and the next year iMessage went end-to-end.

That should have been the end of the capability.

It wasn't. The crypto held and the software around it didn't. Encryption is math, and the math has been effectively unbreakable for civilian purposes for decades. But the math runs inside a parser written in C, next to an image decoder written in C, reachable from a message you never opened and cannot decline to receive. So the surveillance business migrated from intercepting the channel to owning the endpoint, and an entire industry grew into that gap: exploit brokers, vulnerability research boutiques, commercial spyware vendors selling remote compromise as a service to governments who couldn't build it themselves. The 1990s Clipper Chip fight and the 2016 Apple–FBI standoff over San Bernardino were the loud, visible battles. Law enforcement lost them in public and then won anyway, in private, on a purchase order.

The mechanism underneath is a supply story, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. Memory-unsafe code emits exploitable bugs at a fairly steady rate as an ordinary byproduct of shipping features, and every new feature is new attack surface. Mitigations — ASLR, sandboxing, pointer authentication, hardened allocators — didn't stop this; they raised the price, forcing chains of several bugs instead of one. Expensive, yes. Exhausted, never. Exploitable vulnerabilities behaved like a renewable resource with an inflating price tag, which means a government that wanted access could always buy access.

And a government that can always buy access has remarkably little appetite for spending political capital on a backdoor mandate that Silicon Valley, civil liberties groups, and every cryptographer alive will fight to the death.

Picture a boiler with rising pressure and a relief valve nobody installed on purpose. The pressure is the political demand for lawful access — it never went away, it just vented. The valve is the exploit supply. For twenty years the hiss out of that valve was loud enough that the boiler never blew, and everyone in the room mistook the absence of an explosion for an agreement. The analogy breaks in exactly one place, and the break is the frightening part: a real relief valve is engineered, rated, inspected. This one was an accident of C's memory model. Nobody specified it, nobody monitors it, and nobody planned for what happens when it closes.

Now close it. Rust in the Android and Windows kernels. Memory-safe rewrites of the highest-risk parsers, the ones sitting at the end of an attacker-controlled byte stream. Fuzzers and sanitizers running continuously instead of before release. And then, layered on top, AI code review that never gets bored or distracted, applied to every diff in every codebase, plus AI-driven bug hunting that reaches the cheap bugs before the brokers do. Exploits don't need to become impossible for this to bite. They only need to become unaffordable at scale — push the price of a working remote chain high enough and it stops being a routine investigative tool and becomes a strategic weapon spent on a handful of targets a year.

At which point the pressure has nowhere left to go but into legislation.

Which means the last fifteen years taught us something we never said out loud at conferences: cheap exploits, not good policy, are what kept end-to-end encryption legal.

Sit with that for a second.

The two outcomes our field has pursued with equal sincerity — eliminate memory-safety bugs and keep strong encryption for everyone — were never independent goals. A hydraulic link ran between them that nobody drew on the whiteboard, because nobody thought of the bug supply as infrastructure. Winning the security war and losing the privacy war may turn out to be one event with two names.

So what do you do with it? Not despair, and emphatically not sabotage — I want the memory-safe rewrites, all of them, yesterday. The portable lesson is about how to read your own victories. When a fight goes quiet for a long time, ask what is paying for the quiet, because something always is: a subsidy nobody itemized, a resource nobody budgeted, a slow leak relieving pressure while the room congratulated itself on the peace treaty.

And here is what would change my mind. If the exploit valve closes but a new one opens — every private thought routed through someone's inference cluster, plaintext by architecture, because that's where the assistant lives — then the pressure vents again and the mandate fight stays dormant on far worse terms. That isn't a reprieve. That's the same bargain with better branding.

The crypto wars never ended in a ceasefire. They ended in an armistice paid for in bugs, and the money is running out.
