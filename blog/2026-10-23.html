Your Resolver Is One Court Order From Lying to You

Quad9 is the internet's closest thing to a neutral utility. Free. Anonymous. No logs. Billions of queries a day, and no record of who asked for what.

It just announced it's leaving France. Because a French court ordered it to lie to you.

The Case of the Qatari Broadcaster

beIN, a Qatari sports broadcaster with French offices, sued to force Quad9 to stop resolving a list of domains it claims are pirating its broadcasts. Quad9 has no connection to those domains, no relationship with their operators. It's the internet's phone book, not the publisher. The court ordered it to block them anyway, with a penalty of €580,000 per day—later reduced by the appeals court to €1,000 per day. The ruling came down yesterday, October 22. Quad9's service ends in France on November 24.

Now, let's be precise about what DNS actually is, because the entire case hangs on a misunderstanding—one that the French courts shared.

Why DNS Blocking Isn't Like Shutting a Store

DNS is the lookup step your device performs before it opens any connection. You type example.com, your resolver returns an IP address, then your browser talks to that address. Blocking at the DNS layer means returning a bogus answer—or nothing at all—for a specific name.

The court treated this like ordering a bookstore to stop selling a particular book. Put it in the back room. Done. But DNS isn't a store; it's a giant, distributed cache. Every resolver that answers your query is just a cache of records it read from the authoritative servers run by the domain's owner. Quad9 doesn't own any of the names beIN wants blocked. It's a phone book that the phone company also printed—and now the court says the phone company must tear out a page in every copy it hands out.

Here's the mechanism that makes this technically impossible, not just philosophically repellent: Quad9 has no database of users by jurisdiction. Its service is free, open, and anonymous—no sign-up, no identity, no logs. It cannot tell which of its billions of queries come from France and which come from Germany or Canada, because it deliberately does not keep the information that would let it do so.

So the court's order presents a trilemma: obey globally, obey by building surveillance, or refuse and pay penalties until you're dead.

Three Ways to Lose

Option one: global blocking. Quad9 could add those domains to its global threat-blocking database, the same list it uses to filter malware and phishing. That would satisfy the court. It would also impose a French court's decision on every user worldwide—the “extraterritorial” effect the court itself didn't address. One broadcaster's copyright claim becomes internet law in every timezone. That's not a technical choice; it's a political one, made by default.

Option two: geo-targeted blocking. To block only French users, Quad9 would need to know which users are French. That means IP-geolocation lookups, which means per-query logging, which violates its core privacy promise. “There is no way we can determine which users this ruling applies to—we have no list of French users, because we do not keep dossiers of our users at all,” the organization says. Building that dossier is exactly what it refuses to do.

Option three: refuse and eat the fines. The reduced penalty is €1,000 per day until the blocking ends on November 14—about €24,000 total, plus legal fees. Quad9 says it “cannot survive the accumulation of penalties yet to be assessed in 20 other pending cases.” Twenty. This isn't one rogue judge in Paris; it's a pattern.

And that's the thing nobody wants to say out loud.

Every DNS resolver is already a political actor.

The only question is which government writes its blocklist. Quad9 chose to leave rather than choose. But the moment a court in any country issues a blocking order against a resolver that wants to stay, that resolver will have to pick a side—and it will have to break something to do it.

The Myth of Neutrality

The internet's foundational myth is that infrastructure is neutral. Pipes don't care what flows through them. But DNS is not a pipe; it's a lookup table with a gun to its head. The moment any jurisdiction can compel a resolver to alter its answers, that resolver is no longer a neutral utility. It's a political institution with a country's flag on it.

Quad9's exit is the first crack, not the last. Every DNS provider—Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1, Google's 8.8.8.8, OpenDNS—is one law away from the same choice. They might not all make the same call. Some will comply, citing “local law.” Others will block globally, because that's cheaper than building jurisdiction-tracking. The internet's global addressing system will become a patchwork of locally mandated lies.

What This Frees You To See

So what do you do with this? First, stop assuming your resolver is neutral. Ask what its jurisdiction is, where it's incorporated, what its history of fighting or complying with blocking orders looks like. Read the transparency reports—Quad9's is public, and it names the orders it receives.

Second, understand that the battle isn't technical; it's legal and political. The fix has to come from law, not from better cryptography. DNSSEC can sign answers so you know they're authentic, but it can't stop a court from ordering a resolver to not give you the answer at all.

Third, and this is the quiet one: you can't outsource your integrity. If you run any service that crosses borders—a content CDN, an API, a search index—you're in the same position Quad9 is in. One country's court can order you to break something that serves everyone. You will have to decide, in advance, what you're willing to break. Quad9 decided its promise not to log users was non-negotiable. It gave up a country to keep that promise.

Before you judge them fools for leaving, ask yourself: which promise of yours is worth a country?
