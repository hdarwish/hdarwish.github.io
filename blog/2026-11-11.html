Your code is clean; your intent is not

A single patent claim halted Linux display development not by breaking the build, but by breaking the trust.

linuxopen-sourcepatentsdisplaylinklegal

Open source is often treated as a shared commons, but it is actually a legal minefield where the act of understanding a system can be interpreted as an act of infringement.

The distinctionThe distinction between *copyright* (which protects expression) and *patent* (which protects function) is the single most dangerous blind spot in the open-source ecosystem.

01

The RFC that became a lawsuit

Mike Lothian wanted a working dock. His Dell station, powered by a DisplayLink chipset, was crashing under Linux. Rather than wait for a vendor fix, he used an LLM to help write a new driver, Vino, and posted it to the dri-devel mailing list as an RFC. He also opened a GitHub issue on the vendor’s own EVDI repository, asking for help with a specific blocker: the DL3 control-plane sequence.

He got no reply from the developers. Instead, three weeks later, he received an email from Synaptics legal. It didn’t ask for a patch. It didn’t ask for a collaboration. It stated that DisplayLink is not an open standard, that the protocol is proprietary, and that Lothian’s work was moving toward implementing patented technology.

The mechanism here is not code. It is intent. In copyright law, if you write the code from scratch, you are safe. You own the expression. In patent law, if you *understand* the function and replicate it, you are liable. Lothian didn’t copy a line of Synaptics code; he tried to make the hardware work. But to make the hardware work, he had to reverse-engineer the DL3 protocol. That act of understanding is what triggered the legal response.

02

The 125-patent appendix

On August 17, the tone shifted from warning to cease-and-desist. Synaptics sent a notice citing 125 patents. The letter noted that Lothian had not "substantially responded" to the earlier warning and had continued "public statements and further development activities."

The metric that matters here isn’t the number of lines of code Lothian wrote. It’s the chilling effect. One developer, in one country (Scotland, specifically, which explains the UK patent focus), now faces the threat of "expensive legal penalties." The consequence is immediate: work on Vino has stopped. The dri-devel list, a place where thousands of drivers are born, has effectively lost a contributor who is now legally risked.

This is the trap. The "open" in open-source does not mean "open to legal risk." It means "open to inspection." But inspection, when it leads to functional replication of a patented process, is where the legal shield fails.

03

Why the kernel is a target

Synaptics (who acquired DisplayLink in 2022) provides a binary blob for Linux. They do not provide the source for the DL3 codec. They provide an out-of-tree kernel module, EVDI, which is open source but only handles the virtual display interface, not the proprietary compression stream.

This creates a paradox. The hardware is sold to the general public. The software is a closed blob. The kernel driver is a half-open patchwork. When a user tries to fill in the gaps with a clean-room implementation, they are stepping into a zone where the vendor’s legal team sees not a helpful community member, but a potential infringer.

The mechanism of the DL3 protocol involves compressing video, audio, and USB channels into a single stream. To decode it, you must understand the exact sequence of control-plane engagements. Lothian asked for help isolating a "whole-bus timing/ordering property." That is a technical question. Synaptics answered with a legal one.

The turn

The danger is not that Synaptics will sue Lothian into bankruptcy. The danger is that every other developer reading the dri-devel list is now asking: "If I try to fix this driver, will I get a letter?" The patent claim didn't just stop one driver; it froze the trust in the ecosystem. It turned a technical problem into a legal risk. And legal risk is the one thing that no amount of LLM assistance or community goodwill can mitigate.

Analogy Imagine a library that owns the only copy of a book. You are allowed to read it, but if you write a summary of the plot in your own words and publish it, the library can sue you for copyright infringement of the *story*, even though you didn't copy a single sentence.

Where it breaks This breaks down because patent law is even broader than copyright. You don't even need to summarize the story; you just need to achieve the same result using the same method. The library can sue you for *using the book's logic*, not just its words.

If you're deciding
When
You are working on a driver for hardware with a proprietary protocol and a closed-source blob.
Prefer
Assume that any reverse-engineering of the control plane is a legal event, not just a technical one. Check for patent portfolios before posting an RFC.
Avoid
The assumption that "clean-room" code is a safe harbor. It is not. Function is protected, not just expression.

The code was clean. The intent was honest. But in the world of patents, the only thing that matters is the function. And that function belongs to them.
