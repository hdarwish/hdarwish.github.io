Stop Pretending Go Can Teach You Rust's Philosophy

Last year, we considered migrating a project from Go to Rust. Our Go codebase was sprawling with goroutines, and initially, it felt liberating. But then, data races emerged, causing bugs that only appeared in production.

Go's simplicity is its charm. The compiler seems to say, "Don't worry, I've got this." Rust, however, demands attention. It's not just about language features; it's about embracing a different philosophy.

The first time I encountered a panic in Go, a teammate shrugged it off, saying, "Runtime safety is usually good enough." In Rust, this attitude doesn't fly. The compiler is strict, ensuring you don't make mistakes.

Rust's borrow checker enforces this new mindset. It doesn't make life harder; it prevents you from making errors. In Go, goroutines are easy to use but can lead to subtle bugs. Rust's concurrency model, though more demanding, eliminates these issues.

Rust's learning curve is often criticized. It's not just about learning new syntax; it's about changing how you think. You can't carry over Go's casual confidence and expect it to work in Rust's structured environment.

Compile times in Rust are longer than in Go, but each compile acts like a thorough code review. It's like having a meticulous colleague who won't let anything slip through. Annoying, but ultimately beneficial.

The shift from Go to Rust isn't just technical; it's philosophical. It's about taking responsibility for code safety. Once you trust Rust's assurances, it feels liberating.

Is Rust worth the effort? Yes, if you're ready to abandon old habits and embrace a more rigorous approach. Rust's philosophy isn't for everyone, but for those who adapt, it offers freedom from runtime errors and surprises.

To gain more, you must be willing to change more. That's the real lesson from moving to Rust.
