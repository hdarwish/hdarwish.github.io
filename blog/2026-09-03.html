Spam no longer needs to rank. It only needs to exist.

215,128 pages, and none of them were written for you

Two of the sites doing the grounding for AI buying advice have given their homepages the HTML title Facts & Grounding Page. Not "Home." Not "Reviews." Grounding — the retrieval step a model performs before it answers. That's not a slip of the CMS. That's a sign hung on the door for an audience of exactly one kind of reader.

Trellner's TR-2026-009 put 380 buyer-intent categories to two web-grounded Perplexity models, 760 calls, and kept every URL that came back. Of 7,534 citations, 59.8% point at domains ranked worse than #100,000 in Tranco, and 23.4% at domains that aren't in the top million at all. Median Tranco rank of the ranked ones: 71,611. Wikipedia was cited three times.

Three of the cited sites — wifitalents.com, worldmetrics.org, gitnux.org — were registered through NameCheap between December 2023 and May 2024, delegate DNS to the same pair of Cloudflare nameservers, run the same template with the same nav, and each keep a blog of exactly six posts, all eighteen of which are about the other brands in the set. Their sitemaps list 103,578, 107,083 and 105,541 URLs. Of those, 70,731, 71,684 and 72,713 are /best/<something>-software/.

215,128 generated buying guides. Zero of them intended for a human.

What a link used to cost

PageRank made the web adversarial in a specific, expensive way. To rank, you needed inbound edges, and edges live on other people's domains. You could buy them, beg them, farm them, or hack them — but every single one was a transaction with a third party who could refuse, raise the price, or get deindexed and take you down with them. The scarce resource was never content. Content was always free. The scarce resource was other people's endorsements, and search spam was, at bottom, the business of acquiring endorsements below market price.

That cost structure is why link farms were farms: capital-intensive, fragile, worth defending, and staffed. Google's entire counter-strategy was to make the endorsement harder to forge — nofollow, Penguin, manual actions. It worked well enough that spam grew overheads, a churn rate, and a sales team.

Now price the new attack. A grounded answer comes from a pipeline with no opinions in it: crawl the page, split it into chunks, embed each chunk, embed the user's question, return the nearest k. Look for the step where something asks who vouches for this document. There isn't one. There is only distance.

Which means the cheapest way to sit near a question in embedding space is to restate the question. A page headed "Best RFID Software" that lists twenty real RFID vendor names is not merely relevant to "best RFID software" — it is very nearly a copy of it, plus enough surrounding entity vocabulary to look like evidence. Under PageRank you had to be endorsed. Under retrieval you have to be adjacent, and adjacency is something you can type.

So the metric that moved is cost per candidate document. It used to be dominated by buying an edge in a graph you don't control; now it's tokens plus a sitemap line. Fractions of a cent, and falling. And the rational strategy inverts with it: stop fighting for one high-value slot, emit seventy thousand near-duplicate pages, and let one of them be nearest for each of seventy thousand long-tail queries that no human ever bothered to write a real guide for. The three sites turn up in 41 of 380 categories with 181 citations — 2.4% of all evidence in the study — from a standing start after December 2023.

The librarian who only sniffs the spine

Picture two libraries. In the first, you ask for the best book on RFID and the librarian says: three specialists independently cite this one, so start here. She is aggregating other people's judgments. She can absolutely be fooled — but fooling her means fooling the specialists, and the specialists have reputations they'd rather keep.

In the second library, the librarian takes your question, walks the stacks, and hands you whichever spine most closely resembles the words you just said. She is fast, tireless, and completely indifferent to provenance. Print a book titled exactly like every question anyone might ask, and you own her.

The analogy breaks in one honest place, and it's worth naming: Perplexity plainly isn't purely similarity. g2.com took 291 citations and reddit.com 261; gartner.com and capterra.com sit near the top. Some authority signal exists in there. But the top ten domains account for only 17.3% of citations, and 751 of 2,055 cited domains aren't in the top million. Whatever authority weighting exists, it isn't load-bearing. The long tail is where the answers actually come from — and the tail is the part you can manufacture.

The most instructive case in the whole dataset isn't even adversarial. guideflow.com sells interactive product demos. It reviews nothing, competes in none of the 380 categories, and was cited 194 times across 96 of them — third overall, ahead of Gartner. Ninety-six distinct blog URLs, one per category, six of them the Estonian-locale copy. Nobody deceived anybody. A vendor published content marketing, as thousands do, and the retrieval layer quietly promoted it into the third-largest evidence base for what you should buy.

You have already built this machine

If you have shipped anything with a vector store in it during the last two years, you built a retrieval system that scores documents by cosine similarity, applies no authority weighting whatsoever, has no notion of which of your sources is load-bearing and which is somebody's abandoned Confluence export, and treats a Slack rant and a signed architecture decision record as peers so long as they're topically nearby — which means the only thing currently protecting your "trusted corpus" is that nobody has bothered to point seventy thousand plausible documents at it yet.

Search spam had to earn its rank. Citation spam only has to exist.

Sit with that for a second.

Authority is a thing you have to add back

Retrieval gives you relevance, and relevance was never the same quantity as credibility. Similarity is a claim about words. Credibility is a claim about people. The web spent twenty-five years building the second one on top of the first, imperfectly, at enormous cost, in open war with an industry that had payroll. Then we shipped a generation of systems that dropped that layer entirely and called what was left grounding.

So put it back yourself, explicitly, in your own code. For every corpus you retrieve from, you should be able to answer three questions: who can write into this, what does the age distribution of the documents look like, and would I notice ten thousand new plausible ones appearing? Trellner noticed precisely by asking the third: median first Wayback capture 2020 for the unranked cited domains against 2011 for the ranked ones, with 16.6% of archived unranked domains first seen in 2025 or later, versus 1.6%. Domain age is not truth. But provenance metadata you can rank on beats a similarity score you cannot argue with.

The old spam war was expensive because endorsement was expensive. Retrieval made the ammunition free, and the shooting has already started in the cheapest possible place: a page whose title says, out loud, that it was written for a machine.
