The creative class isn't dying. It's splitting.

Two hundred thousand creative jobs vanished in four years. But the real story isn't AI — it's that the industry is restructuring into a two-tier system of AI-augmented elites and disposable gig workers.

AIcreative economylabormediastructural change

The creative economy has always been a pyramid, not a ladder. But AI is not just eroding the base — it's accelerating a split that was already underway: a thin layer of stars and studios who own the means of production, and a vast, replaceable workforce of gig workers who never had much leverage to begin with.

The distinctionThe relevant distinction is not 'AI vs. human' but 'owners of the means of production vs. those who rent their labor to it.' The question 'how much is AI?' obscures the structural shift in who captures the value.

01 / 04

The numbers that should make you suspicious

Two hundred thousand creative jobs lost in four years. Fifty thousand in the last year alone. The movie and sound recording sector — Hollywood — shed more than a hundred thousand jobs, nearly a third of its workforce, the worst stretch in thirty years. That's the kind of collapse you'd expect from a recession. There is no recession.

The obvious suspect is AI, and the media has been asking 'how much is AI to blame?' for months. But the question is wrong. It assumes AI is a force that arrived from outside and started pushing people out of their jobs. The data tells a different, stranger story.

Look at the split. Every digital subsector — publishing, broadcasting, graphic design, streaming — is losing jobs. But in-person entertainment is still growing. Live theater, concerts, museums, theme parks: fine. The job losses are concentrated in the industries that can be digitized and distributed at near-zero marginal cost.

That's not a coincidence. It's a clue.

02 / 04

The internet already did this once

To understand what's happening now, you have to look at the first digital disruption. The source article reminds us: when Napster arrived, Metallica sued, won, and bankrupted the service. But they won the battle and lost the war. The internet made copying music trivial, and no lawsuit could put that genie back in the bottle.

The result wasn't the death of music. It was a massive transfer of value. The price of a single Metallica album in 2000 now buys you a month of access to nearly all human-made music. For consumers, this is a miracle. For musicians, it's an economic catastrophe — streaming revenue has never come close to the CD-era paydays, even before adjusting for inflation.

And that's the key: the internet didn't just make music cheaper. It changed what music was for. Music became a loss-leading advertisement for live shows, merchandise, and brand deals. The value didn't disappear — it moved. And it moved away from the people who made the music and toward the platforms that distributed it.

03 / 04

This time, the workers are already disposable

Now look at the current creative economy. It's not a ladder where you start at the bottom and climb to the top. It's a pyramid with a very thin top — a few thousand people who own IP, studios, and platforms — and a very wide base of gig workers, freelancers, and contract staff who have always been treated as replaceable.

The source article notes that media firms are 'decidedly coy' about their AI use, both to avoid public backlash and to protect their copyrights. But the real tell is what they're doing: they're not laying off the people who own the ideas. They're laying off the people who execute them — the editors, the sound engineers, the junior designers, the staff writers.

AI didn't cause this. It just made it cheaper to do what the industry was already doing: treating creative labor as a cost to be minimized, not a value to be invested in. The two-tier system is the natural endpoint of a business model that has been squeezing the middle for decades.

04 / 04

The question that would actually matter

The media keeps asking 'how much is AI to blame?' because it's a clean, quantifiable question. But it's the wrong one. The right question is: who is capturing the value that AI creates?

When a studio uses AI to generate a script, the value doesn't vanish. It flows to the studio that owns the output. When a platform uses AI to recommend content, the value flows to the platform. The workers who used to do that work are not the ones who benefit.

The source article's own framing is telling: it asks 'Can the Arts Business Model Survive?' But the business model is surviving just fine. Hollywood is still making billions. The top earners are making more than ever. What's dying is the middle — the staff jobs, the stable careers, the path from gig to tenure.

That's not a technology problem. It's a power problem.

The turn

The real story isn't that AI is stealing jobs. It's that the creative class was already being restructured into a two-tier system — a thin layer of AI-augmented elites who own the means of production, and a disposable gig workforce that has never had much leverage. AI didn't create that split. It just made it cheaper to accelerate it.

Analogy The creative economy is like a farm that used to need a hundred field hands. The tractor didn't kill the farm — it just meant the farm needed ten people who owned tractors and ninety people who owned nothing.

Where it breaks The analogy breaks because a tractor is a tool anyone can buy. AI is a tool that is owned by the platforms that deploy it, and the 'field hands' don't get to buy the AI — they get to rent it from the same people who are replacing them.

If you're deciding
When
You are a creative professional watching your industry shed jobs and wondering if you're next.
Prefer
Ask who owns the output. Invest in ownership — IP, audience, a distinctive voice — not just skill.
Avoid
The trap is to keep optimizing for employability within a system that is actively devaluing your labor. 'Learn AI tools' is not a strategy; it's a survival tactic.

The question isn't 'how much is AI to blame?' It's 'who gets to own the machine?' Answer that, and you'll know what the creative class looks like in ten years.
