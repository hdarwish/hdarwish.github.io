A law demanding the impossible is a trap, not a law

Utah's new VPN law would have required adult websites to be both blind and omniscient at the same time. A federal court just called that what it is: a technical impossibility, on pain of legal liability.

Feel the trap. A platform that cannot tell whether a visitor is in Salt Lake City or Shanghai has exactly two choices: verify everyone's age, everywhere, forever, or risk violating the law the moment one obfuscated user slips through. The court's ruling blocks the law, but the deeper scandal isn't the constitutionality. It's that the state demanded something the internet cannot do.

What Utah actually tried to do

SB 73, signed into law earlier this year, required adult websites to block VPN users or to identify the physical location of every visitor using a VPN or similar traffic-masking tool. It even banned websites from telling users how to use a VPN to bypass the checks. To EFF's knowledge, that made Utah the first state in the nation to explicitly target VPN use to evade age-verification gates.

Aylo, the parent company of Pornhub and others, sued immediately. The court paused enforcement while compliance rules were drafted. Those proposed rules were published September 1, with a possible effective date of October 8. Then Judge Barlow stepped in.

His preliminary injunction didn't just say the law was burdensome. It said the statute "requires entities like Aylo to geolocate its website users with perfection to avoid liability" — and then acknowledged, plainly, that "geolocation perfection is not presently possible."

Why geolocation perfection is a fantasy

To see why "perfection" is a fantasy, watch the path of one packet. When you connect to a VPN, your traffic doesn't go straight to the destination website. It goes from your device to a VPN server — maybe in another state, another country, another continent — and only then to the site you're visiting.

To the destination server, the connection appears to come from the VPN server's IP address, not yours. That's the entire point of the tool. The website sees a connection from Seattle, or Frankfurt, or Tokyo, and it has no reliable way to know whether the human on the other end is actually in Utah.

Think of it like a letter with no return address. The postmark tells you which post office handled it, not where the sender sat down to write. VPNs are the post office that stamps a different city on every envelope — and the destination has no way to peel the stamp off and read the original ink.

The analogy breaks, though, because a VPN isn't lying. It's doing exactly what it promises: hiding the source. The website isn't being deceived; it's being given incomplete information, by design, with the user's consent.

So the metric that matters here isn't latency or throughput. It's location certainty — and VPNs reduce it to effectively zero for any individual connection. The consequence for a platform is stark: you can't know if a visitor is in Utah, so you can't know if you're violating the law. The only way to avoid liability is to treat every visitor as potentially in Utah, which means age-verifying the entire planet.

The court's arithmetic

Judge Barlow did the math that the legislature skipped. In his ruling, he noted that the law's "actual-location provision in practice requires an entity to perform age verification services for every user visiting its site from any location because the entity would violate the law if even one of those users happened to be obfuscating."

That's not a small burden. That's an infinite one.

Picture a site in France, serving a user in Japan, and that user happens to be using a VPN. Under the law's literal reading, the site is now in violation of Utah law. It didn't know. It couldn't know. And still, it's liable.

This is the moment the trap snaps shut. Every VPN user, every TLS handshake that hides its endpoints, every encrypted packet that obscures its payload — all of it already violates the law's literal reading. The state wasn't regulating behavior. It was outlawing the internet itself.

A law demanding a technical impossibility is not a law — it's a trap.

Sit with what that means. Every ordinary act of privacy becomes a potential violation. Every platform becomes a hostage to information it can never possess.

This isn't a Utah problem, or a VPN problem, or an adult-content problem. It's a pattern. Regulators look at a technology, see something they don't like, and write a rule that assumes the technology can do something it can't. The internet has no central chokepoint where location can be verified with certainty. It never has. And no statute can change that.

What this frees you to do

Before you comply with a law that demands certainty, ask what certainty would even mean in your system. If the answer is "nothing," then the law isn't asking you to comply. It's asking you to be a surveillance apparatus for a fiction.

You are not obligated to build the impossible, and you are not obligated to pretend you can. The court's ruling is a reminder that the laws of networking are not suggestions — they are constraints that no legislature can override. You can route around a bad law, but you cannot route around physics.

The next time someone hands you a compliance requirement that demands perfection, remember the letter with no return address. You can't read what isn't there. And a law that demands you do isn't a law at all.
