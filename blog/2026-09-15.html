SOAP Survived Because Commerce Needs a Constitution

A supposedly dead protocol quietly carries Brazil’s electronic fiscal documents between businesses and tax authorities.

SOAP survived here by becoming much more than an envelope: mutual TLS with an e-CNPJ certificate, precisely shaped XML, document-specific signatures, multiple authorizing environments, and enough regional variation to make “just call the API” sound like something said moments before discovering that paperwork has achieved sentience. None of this is fashionable. All of it is load-bearing.

The protocol nobody gets to replace casually

Fiscal infrastructure cannot optimize for the pleasure of its newest client library. An interface connecting businesses, tax authorities, transport operators, states, and national services must preserve identity, validation rules, document semantics, and compatibility across organizations that do not upgrade together. A fashionable API may reduce effort inside one codebase; a durable contract reduces coordination cost across an economy.

Those are not the same optimization.

A map of the actual machine

The sefaz-webservices-postman project makes this machinery unusually visible. Its generated Postman collection contains 29 items in five folders, covering NF-e model 55, NFC-e model 65, CT-e model 57, MDF-e model 58, and event references. The collection is not maintained as a heap of hand-edited requests: gen_inventario.py produces inventario.json, then build_collection.py produces the Postman JSON. When endpoints, versions, and envelopes form a large compatibility surface, generation turns configuration drift into reviewable source changes.

All these services use SOAP 1.2, but “all use SOAP” does not mean “all work alike.” The action travels in the Content-Type rather than a SOAPAction header. Authorization, event, and invalidation requests require XMLDSig signatures, which Postman itself does not create; status, query, and distribution requests do not. Client authentication uses an e-CNPJ certificate over mutual TLS. Each layer answers a different question: who may connect, who signed the document, what operation is intended, and whether the authority recognizes its shape.

SOAP here resembles a standardized shipping container. The container does not simplify the cargo; it gives ports a stable shape to lift, inspect, route, and reject, even when every ship and warehouse belongs to a different operator. The analogy breaks because these envelopes also participate in identity and legal validation, whereas a steel box is gloriously indifferent to whether its owner signed the bananas.

The differences inside those envelopes are where developer taste loses its vote. CT-e distribution uses distDFeInt with cUFAutor, wrapped inside cteDistDFeInteresse and cteDadosMsg, without cabecMsg. MDF-e distribution omits cUFAutor from that payload, puts cUF in mdfeCabecMsg, and places mdfeDadosMsg directly in the body. NF-e distribution resembles CT-e and uses version 1.01. These are not cosmetic dialects that a generic serializer can politely ignore; they determine which authority can parse and accept the request.

One misplaced field changes the verdict.

The contracts encode operational policy as well as document shape. CT-e 4.00 authorization is synchronous, and the national service list has neither asynchronous batches nor invalidation. MDF-e 3.00 authorization sends its data area as GZip plus base64. NFC-e requires CSC and a QR Code, supports offline contingency through tpEmis=9, and several states operate their own environments. Those choices change payload representation, request flow, and endpoint selection, so an integration must model each fiscal document rather than conceal everything behind one suspiciously cheerful send().

The repository also refuses the comforting fiction that one catalogue is the whole country. Its defaults cover SVRS, which serves most states, plus the national distribution environments; it does not catalogue all 27 states individually. An authority-specific service such as NFC-e in São Paulo requires changing the URL. The repository records that CT-e and MDF-e distribution were validated against production on 12 September 2026 and returned cStat 137. The meaningful metric is therefore not synthetic elegance or a tiny client wrapper, but verified contract coverage: which exact envelope reached which real authority and received a recognized result.

The legacy is the coordination

If replacing this network meant changing only XML into another serialization format, modernization would be straightforward; instead, the format is braided together with certificate identity, XML signatures, document models, event codes, state authority boundaries, production and homologation endpoints, synchronous behavior, compressed payloads, and the accumulated expectation that independent organizations can continue understanding one another without upgrading in lockstep.

Legacy protocols survive because institutions need contracts, not elegance.

A constitution is an imperfect but useful analogy. SOAP does not decide fiscal policy; authorities and specifications do. Yet the network makes portions of that policy executable: a message has a permitted shape, a recognized signer, an intended authority, and a mechanically checkable result. What looks like protocol cruft from inside one application is often settled coordination when viewed across every participant.

Modernize around the promise

The portable lesson is not “use SOAP.” Please do not turn an observation into a hostage situation. The better question is: what promise is this ugly interface keeping? Ask which parties upgrade independently, which identities must be proven, which variants reflect jurisdiction rather than accident, and which invalid messages must be rejected in the same way by strangers who share no release schedule.

Then modernize the parts that do not carry that promise. Generate collections from one inventory. Wrap certificate handling without pretending it has vanished. Test exact envelopes against homologation and, where appropriate, production. Improve tooling around the contract before replacing the contract itself, because otherwise “simplification” merely moves coordination risk from the protocol onto every organization forced to interpret its successor.

An elegant interface is pleasant to read. A durable one lets strangers keep trading.
