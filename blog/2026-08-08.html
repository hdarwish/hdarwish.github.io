The knitting needles are the whole argument

A man on a commuter train spends over half an hour narrating EBITDA, margin expansion, cost structure and ARR into his AirPods, in a monotone so flat that a stranger across the shared table remembers it as physically painful. Then the brakes screech, everyone stands, and he digs frantically through a leather bag. Out come two knitting needles and a mound of pink yarn. A winter hat for his niece. And only then — needles in hand, yarn dangling — does the stranger notice a glint of pride in his eyes.

By every economic measure available, the hat is the worse object. It is slower to make, less valuable, less scalable, and a machine could produce a better one before he finishes the brim. The call, meanwhile, was plausibly moving real capital around real companies. He knows all this. He is proud of the hat anyway.

That inversion is worth taking seriously, because it isn't sentimentality. It's a measurement failure, and a great many knowledge workers are currently discovering it inside themselves. It explains the specific flavor of despair Aaron Horwath describes in Noema: professionals in low-lit bars trading fantasies about goat farms and surf instruction, senior people with decades of institutional knowledge suddenly unsure the edifice was ever load-bearing. The usual story is “AI is coming for the jobs.” The usual story is badly under-specified.

For most knowledge work, nobody can directly observe value. You cannot see whether the deck changed the decision, whether the refactor prevented the outage that never happened, whether the memo was read by anyone who mattered. The causal chain from your Tuesday to the company's fortunes runs through too many other people to trace. So the organization measures the nearest observable proxy: effort. Hours logged, difficulty absorbed, fluency under pressure, the visible strain of a competent person doing something hard. This is why promotion packets read like endurance records, why a reviewer instinctively respects a large diff, why the whiteboard interview survives every critique of it — all of them are effort meters wearing a value badge.

And the proxy wasn't stupid. It worked for decades, because effort and value really were correlated. Hard things took long; long things were hard. If you could produce a coherent forty-slide diligence document in a week, that fact alone certified you, and nobody needed to check the slides.

Effort was the collateral. Value was the loan.

A currency pegged to gold behaves the same way: the peg holds as long as nobody tests it at scale, and while it holds, the paper is the gold for every practical purpose. You can price things in it, pay salaries in it, feel rich in it. The analogy breaks in one place, and the break is the interesting part — currencies come off the peg by decree, all at once, announced by someone with a name. Nobody decreed this one. The peg between effort and value simply stopped clearing, one deliverable at a time, on a few million individual laptops.

What a language model does, precisely, is decouple those two variables. It emits the artifact without the strain. The deck, the memo, the summary, the boilerplate service, the migration script, the quarterly narrative — the class of thing that used to cost a skilled person three days now costs twenty minutes and no visible suffering. Which forces a comparison your entire career has structurally protected you from: the artifact, alone, with the effort peeled off. Is it good? Did anyone need it? Would its absence have been noticed by a single human being?

For an uncomfortable fraction of the corpus, the honest answers are: fine, no, and no.

Engineers feel this hardest, and the reason is almost cruel. Every other knowledge discipline has already absorbed the accusation of theater — consultants, middle managers, brand strategists all live with a low-grade cultural suspicion that their output is ceremonial, and they've built defenses. Engineering was the exemption. It compiled or it didn't. The tests passed or they didn't. Craft was supposedly the one thing immune to the critique, because it touched something that could actually fail. Then the machine started producing the CRUD layer, the adapter, the fixture, the third variation of the same service — and it turned out that a large share of a perfectly healthy codebase had never been craft at all. It was ritual with a linter.

So the thing going around isn't fear of replacement. Replacement is an old fear with a known shape; people have survived outsourcing and offshoring and recessions, and they know the moves. This is different, and worse, because it doesn't come for your income first. It comes for the story that made the income feel earned. If effort was the thing that made the work feel meaningful, then automating the effort isn't liberation.

It's an audit.

And most artifacts fail the audit.

Sit with that before you make the obvious next move, which is to conclude the work was pointless. That is not what the audit shows. AI didn't make knowledge work meaningless. It removed the effort that was disguising which parts already were — and disguise is not the same as content.

Which is, if you can stand it, extraordinarily good news, because an audit is only devastating when you can't act on the findings. You can. You now hold a diagnostic no previous generation of knowledge worker could run: for any piece of your work, ask what remains once effort is subtracted from it. Some things survive that subtraction beautifully. Deciding what the system should be. Noticing the requirement is internally incoherent. Holding an interface stable while the world thrashes underneath it. Killing the feature. Talking to the person whose actual problem was never written on the ticket. None of these get cheaper when generation gets cheap, and several get dearer, because the supply of plausible artifacts just exploded and someone has to know which ones are true.

The hat passes the audit trivially. It survives the subtraction of effort because it was never about the effort — he could have bought a hat, warmer and cheaper, in eleven minutes. Someone specific wanted this one, from him, and no substitute is the same object. That's what value looks like once you strip the performance off. Smaller than a career. Much harder to fake.

Go find the part of your work that would still matter if it appeared instantly, fully formed, at no cost to you. That part is yours. Everything else was yarn.
