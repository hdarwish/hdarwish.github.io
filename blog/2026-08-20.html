Your sin() function undoes the pi you just multiplied in

Open a fast AVX2 implementation of sin and look at the very first thing it does — before any polynomial, before any bit-twiddling. It multiplies the input by 1.27323954473516. That constant has a name you already know. It is 4/pi.

Now look at the calling code. Casey Muratori did this the honest way: he opened the Godot Engine source, searched for tau, and found lines like Math::sin(h * Math_TAU), where h was already a value periodic on 0 to 1. The caller multiplies pi in. The library's opening move is to divide pi back out. Two programmers, one round trip, zero information gained.

That wasted multiply is the part everyone notices, and it is the least interesting thing wrong here.

The unit you pick at a function boundary is not a style preference that costs you one instruction. It is a decision about which numbers in your domain are permitted to be exact, and which are condemned to be approximations for the rest of the program's life. For angles, you almost certainly picked the second option — for the angles you use most.

Start with how sin actually works, because it's simpler than the mystique around it. No implementation evaluates a series across the whole real line; the polynomial degree would be absurd and the error at large arguments unbounded. Instead it reduces the argument into a small canonical interval, evaluates a short polynomial there, then patches up the sign and the sin/cos swap depending on which octant you landed in. The 4/pi multiply is that reduction: the integer part becomes an octant index, the fractional part becomes the local angle. Which tells you something the documentation never does. The library's internal unit of angle was never radians.

It was fractions of a turn all along.

So the caller had turns. The library wanted turns. Radians existed only in the handshake between them — like two people who both speak Portuguese conducting the meeting in Latin because that's what the letterhead says.

And the handshake isn't free, either. Because 4/pi isn't representable in binary floating point, the reduction constant is itself slightly wrong, and that error gets multiplied by your argument — so for large inputs the octant index can come out flatly incorrect unless the library carries a multi-word approximation of pi and runs something like Payne–Hanek reduction to recover the low bits. An entire subfield of numerical craft exists to undo a unit conversion nobody wanted. In turns, argument reduction is: take the fractional part. Exact. Free.

Muratori's fix is embarrassingly small. Make sin and cos take turns, which mostly means changing one constant, and if you need legacy callers, keep the radian versions as thunks that divide by tau on the way in. The caller saves a multiply. The library gets an exact reduction. Nobody loses.

If you feel the old math-class dread that this is somehow cheating — it isn't. A turn is a real, named unit: 0 is 0°, 0.5 is 180°, 1 is 360°, 2 is 720°. Nothing in mathematics decreed that sine must eat radians. Radians are convenient because the derivative of sin comes out clean at that scaling, which matters enormously in calculus and almost never in the ninety lines of code where you are rotating a sprite. Even the hardware vendors have quietly conceded the point: CUDA ships sincospi, parameterized on half turns, a full circle being [0, 2].

Now ask what a quarter turn costs to store. In turns it's 0.25 — a power of two, a bit pattern that consumes zero bits of mantissa. Half turn is 0.5: also zero. Three quarters is 0.75: one bit. These are the angles your code actually uses, relentlessly, in every axis-aligned rotation, every symmetry test, every UI animation that snaps to a corner. In radians, each of them — pi/2, pi, 3pi/4 — is irrational, and therefore inexact in float32, inexact in float64, inexact in float128, inexact in every binary float you will ever be handed. Not merely worse. Never exact, at any width, forever.

The multiply was never the tax. The multiply was the receipt.

The actual charge is that you took the values your domain cares about most, values that were exactly representable at the call site, and pushed them through a boundary that guaranteed they would arrive slightly wrong — and then you spent the rest of the program comparing them, accumulating them, and wondering why a right angle doesn't quite close.

Radians are an API convention, not a law of mathematics, and both sides of the call pay the fee.

The pattern runs far past trigonometry, and that's the part worth carrying out of here. Every unit at an interface boundary is a claim about which values deserve to be exact. Store money in floating dollars and you have decided that $0.10 is a rounding problem; store it in integer cents and it's a fact. Store time in seconds-as-double and midnight-plus-a-millisecond is an approximation; store it in integer nanoseconds and it's exact until the year 2262. Keep angles in degrees and a third of a turn — 120° — is exact, which is a large part of why degrees survived so long for human-facing work. The unit is a lattice. Your data either lands on the lattice points or drifts between them.

The analogy I keep reaching for is a musical key. You can play any melody in any key, but the fingerings, the accidentals, the notes you fumble at tempo — those belong to the key, not to the music. Pick the wrong one and you spend the whole piece fighting sharps. Where it breaks: transposition in music is lossless, and float conversion is not, which makes the programming case strictly worse than the musical one. You don't get to transpose back.

So when you next design a function that takes a quantity, don't ask what unit is conventional. Ask which values in your domain are sacred — the quarter turn, the exact cent, the tick boundary — and choose the unit that makes those values free. Convention is just somebody else's constraints, viewed from the outside, long after their machine stopped mattering.

Pi never asked to be in your rotation code. Let it go home to geometry, where it earns its keep.
