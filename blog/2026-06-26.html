USB-C Simplicity Is a Myth: Framework's 10G Ethernet Module Shows Why

I read Jeff Geerling's post about the Framework 10G Ethernet module and it highlighted the hidden complexities of USB-C that most users never see. The promise of USB-C is simplicity and universality. One port for everything. But anyone who's tried to get full speed from a high-performance peripheral knows it's not that simple.

Framework's 10G Ethernet module is a prime example. It fits into Framework's expansion slot, using USB-C to connect to the mainboard. This should be seamless. Instead, it reveals the bandwidth complexities and compatibility issues that USB-C often hides.

The Realtek RTL8159 Ethernet controller in the module is impressive on paper, needing USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 (20 Gbps) to reach its full 10 Gbps speed. But achieving these speeds is more wishful thinking than reality. Jeff's tests showed Windows managing 9.4 Gbps, while Linux struggled even more.

The discrepancy comes from how USB standards are implemented across devices. Framework's port documentation claims USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 support on certain ports, but bandwidth often falls short. It's not just the port and controller—it's the interaction of drivers, operating systems, and hardware quirks.

This isn't just a Framework issue. It's a USB-C issue. Plugging in and expecting advertised speeds often leads to disappointment. USB-C is supposed to simplify, but it often requires deep dives into specs and compatibility matrices.

Then there's the heat. The module runs hot—Jeff measured temperatures near 70°C. Compliant with safety standards or not, that's concerning for anyone using it on their lap. Chips running at thermal limits isn't uncommon, but in a form factor about convenience and portability, it's frustrating.

I've seen this before. New interfaces promise universal ease, but under the hood, they're complex. When Thunderbolt first appeared, the promise was similar. Yet, implementation often meant compatibility headaches and limited accessory options.

USB-C is the latest in this cycle. The port is everywhere, yet consistent performance is elusive. It's often a guessing game—will this device deliver the speeds I need, or will I hit a bottleneck?

The lesson here? It's tempting to blame the device, but the real issue is how we understand and market these technologies. USB-C can perform amazingly, but calling it simple is misleading. It requires understanding what's under the hood.

For those relying on high-speed peripherals, it's crucial to do your homework. Check port specs, understand chipset capabilities, and don't assume that just because something fits, it will perform as expected. USB-C is more a starting point than a solution.

Framework's Ethernet module is a case study in tech marketing vs. reality. The promise of plug-and-play with top-tier speeds sounds great, but it's more aspiration than guarantee. Until USB-C's complexity is made predictable, we'll keep hitting these roadblocks.

If these issues sound familiar, it's because they are. We chase the dream of universal connectors, only to find ourselves deep in spec sheets and driver updates. This isn't a call to abandon USB-C, but a reminder to approach it with eyes wide open.

In the end, knowing what you're dealing with is half the battle. If you need consistent high performance, sometimes older, simpler connections are still your best bet. The shiny new thing isn't always the right tool for the job.

Next time you plug in a USB-C device and don't get what you expect, remember: simplicity often hides complexity. With USB-C, that's especially true.
