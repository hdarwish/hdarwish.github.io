Coding Got Better, Software Didn't

Last week, I was knee-deep in a deployment fiasco that spiraled out of control. We were sure we had cracked the nut on 'code quality.' Yet, everything imaginable broke.

I read a post questioning why software seems to be getting worse, despite advances in coding practices. It hit home. We've supposedly mastered coding, but every update feels like defusing a bomb.

The issue is that while coding has improved, software environments have outpaced our tools. Complexity is the real problem, not incompetence.

Consider my latest nightmare. We updated our CI pipeline for 'improvements.' It introduced a latency—a solid 30% increase. Deployments were suddenly taking 15 minutes instead of 10, and on-call hours doubled. The more we fix, the more brittle our system becomes.

This isn't just about agile processes and CI/CD pipelines. It's about expectations. The market demands features, not stability. Companies aim for flashy demos over boring reliability.

Despite having AI that can refactor code in milliseconds, bugs persist. AI can squash them, but it doesn’t dictate priorities. That’s a human job.

I've seen us sacrifice 'boring' optimizations for 'exciting' features. The result? A bloated codebase, ticking like a time bomb. Each event feels more like a high-wire act than an improvement.

It’s not yesterday’s devs or old techniques at fault. They knew less complexity. Today, even the simplest app interacts with an insane number of services and APIs. Each update feels like Russian roulette. Take microservices—they're great for scaling, but managing the interaction between 100+ services? That's like herding cats on caffeine.

Even the best cloud architecture won't save you if stability isn't prioritized. AI tools raise the bar, but they also raise management's expectations.

Teams are squeezed for more with less. The 'do it all' miracle is a false promise. The reality? Fractured focus and cut corners.

Then there’s the human factor. In our rush to adopt new technologies, we often overlook the learning curves they entail. Kubernetes, for instance, promises orchestration bliss, but configuring it feels like navigating a labyrinth. A misconfigured pod can cascade into downtime—just ask the poor soul who spent an entire weekend diagnosing a single missing environment variable.

There’s a path forward. We need to redefine what success means in software.

Imagine if teams took a month, just once, to fix bugs rather than ship features. Imagine metrics that valued uptime over shiny new buttons. But that requires a culture shift, not a tool upgrade.

End the feature fetish. Prioritize robustness. Make stability the thrilling headline.

Until we do, every new software update is another game of chance. Coding may be solved in isolation, but software? That's an ecosystem we still don't manage effectively.

So here's my takeaway: Complexity is here to stay. We're not failing at coding; we're failing at dealing with what comes after. Our task isn't just to build—it's to simplify. And that might be the hardest challenge yet.

Let's pick our battles wisely. It's more than code—it's culture. Maybe next time I'm called at 3am, it'll be for something we couldn't control, not something we chose to ignore.
