Your Neural Net Isn't a Black Box. It's Unfurnished.

For a decade, the story went like this: neural networks are inscrutable knots. Concepts get folded together, packed arbitrarily, hidden in high-dimensional shadows. The only way to study them was to train a second model — a sparse autoencoder — to approximate what the network might look like if it weren't so tangled. We built an entire industry on the premise that the network itself is unreadable.

That premise, it turns out, was a comfortable lie. The mess was never in the networks. It was in our tools.

The Cat-Face Neuron That Started a Religion

The canonical example of polysemanticity — a neuron that does multiple unrelated jobs — was a neuron in an image model that responded to both cat faces and the fronts of cars. That single neuron, plus the last layer of InceptionV1, where almost nothing was interpretable, convinced a generation of researchers that networks pack concepts arbitrarily.

The leading explanation was superposition: when a network runs out of neurons, it stops grouping related things and starts folding unrelated things together, simply because they don't interfere. The result is a knot. You can't study the knot directly — you have to unfold it first, which means training a decomposition (like a sparse autoencoder) and studying that instead.

That was the plan. It was coherent, it was productive, and it was wrong.

What Actually Happened in InceptionV1

When Goodfire's researchers revisited InceptionV1 with modern tools, they found something anticlimactic: the late layers weren't superposed — they were overflowing. Architectural problems had made the layers overflow, and weak interpretability tools amplified the incoherence, making the mess look like a deep mystery when it was really just a plumbing failure.

Here's the kicker. With ordinary architectural improvements — no change to model size, no change to training data — 99–100% of neurons in that last layer became easily interpretable. The network became broadly legible the whole way through. It's the first non-trivial network the author says he mostly understands.

That's not a small finding. That's a demolition of the founding myth.

Recursive Generalization, Not Superposition

So what was the car feature doing spread across dog neurons? The authors call it recursive generalization. Nothing reads that combination back out. The network wasn't hiding the car in a high-dimensional safe. It was just doing ordinary composition — the same way a sentence about a car dealership uses the word "car" in a context that overlaps with "dog" if you're reading a story about a dog in a car.

The canonical circuit that started the superposition idea — found in 2019 — was, by the authors' own admission, the only one they ever saw. And it was misread. No one has published a second circuit with the right shape for superposition.

Think of it like a messy desk. Superposition said the desk is a pile of unrelated objects fused together, and you need a special machine to separate them. The actual finding: the desk is a bit cluttered, but every object is right there. You just need better lighting.

The analogy breaks, of course — a desk doesn't compute. But the point holds: the network's own mechanisms are its legible building blocks.

The Mess Was Never in the Networks

Here's the turn. If the network is clean, then interpretability stops being a philosophical problem and becomes an engineering problem. You don't need to invent a new theory of representation to understand a model. You need better tools, better architecture, and better attention to the ordinary failures of learning.

That's a brutal demotion for a research program that spent a decade building ever-more-elaborate reconstruction objectives. But it's also a gift: it means we can study networks directly, run experiments at agentic scale, and actually understand what's happening.

The mess was never in the networks — it was in our tools.

What This Changes About Alignment

If networks are clean, then alignment is no longer about wrestling with an alien mind that hides its intentions in superposition. It's about reading the circuits — which means we can audit them, test them, and fix them like any other engineering artifact.

That's not to say alignment becomes easy. A trillion-parameter model is still a lot of circuits to read. But it changes the question from "Can we ever understand these things?" to "How do we build the IDE for a trillion-parameter mind?"

The authors' work suggests the answer is: start with the network itself, not with a hypothesized feature list. Build tools that surface the network's own structure, then let the explanations emerge. That's the path to normal science — rapid, iterative, legible.

So the next time someone tells you a neural network is a black box, ask them if they've looked at the neurons with modern tools. The box was never locked. The latch was just rusty.
