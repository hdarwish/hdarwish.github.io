Your Key Size Is Secretly a Deadline

A Certificate Authority can be dead for decades and still confess exactly when its security margin vanished.

In March 1999, Netscape 4.51 shipped two E-Certify root certificates with 512-bit RSA keys: one trusted for SSL, the other for S/MIME. In 2026, Matthew McPherrin recovered both private keys by factoring their public moduli on a Ryzen 9 5950X desktop. The SSL root took 32 hours; the S/MIME root took 29.

The corpse can still sign

This is mostly harmless cryptographic archaeology. Netscape removed the roots in 2002, they expired on 2003-10-16, and they matter now only to someone running Netscape 4.51 with its clock pushed backward. Yet the experiment exposes a mistake that still sneaks into design reviews: we discuss key sizes as properties—“512-bit,” “2048-bit,” “strong”—when they are wagers about how long an attack will remain too expensive to bother with.

Nothing about the public keys changed between 1999 and 2026.

What changed was the price of reversing them. An RSA public key includes a modulus made by multiplying two secret primes. Multiplication is cheap; recovering those primes from their product is the hard direction. McPherrin used CADO-NFS to factor each modulus, then used the recovered primes to reconstruct the corresponding private key.

That reconstruction does not merely reveal an embarrassing secret. A relying system accepts a certificate chain because it can verify that a trusted root authorized the certificates beneath it; possession of the root’s private key lets an attacker manufacture that authorization. For Netscape 4.51, McPherrin could create fresh certificates carrying signatures from an authority the browser already trusted. The certificate’s authority was never mystical. It was an assumption about who could compute a particular signature.

The trust is historical. The forgery is fresh.

“Large” moves while the key stands still

A key size is like a lease on computational infeasibility. During the lease, recovering the secret costs more than the attacker is willing or able to spend; after it, the same public material becomes an instruction sheet for an affordable attack. The analogy breaks at the important part: an ordinary lease prints its end date, while a cryptographic lease depends on future algorithms, hardware, energy prices, parallelism, implementation skill, and the value of whatever the key protects.

E-Certify’s lease was dubious even when signed. Later in 1999, the 512-bit RSA-155 challenge number was factored. These roots were not defeated solely by twenty-seven years of progress; they were already standing near the edge of public capability. Netscape’s eventual removal of them was therefore part of the security mechanism, not administrative tidying, because deleting a trust anchor limits how long a newly affordable attack remains useful.

That distinction matters. Expiration written inside a certificate tells conforming software when to stop accepting it, but expiration does not erase copies, update abandoned clients, rotate embedded roots, or make an old private key mathematically unrecoverable. A date field is a request to the ecosystem. Removal is the ecosystem actually obeying.

The same moving boundary appears farther up the scale. The Web PKI deprecated 1024-bit RSA more than a decade ago. McPherrin notes that no known 1024-bit key has been factored, although such a computation may be within reach of a government or another organization able to coordinate many machines. The recently factored RSA-260 challenge number contains 862 bits—the largest factorization cited in the source—while current systems use at least 2048-bit RSA.

Those numbers are not successive grades of eternal safety. They are snapshots of an economic frontier.

Picture an attacker examining a modulus and declining to factor it. That refusal does not prove the mathematics has protected you forever; it says the expected reward does not justify today’s bill. Better algorithms and cheaper computation push the bill downward, while archived ciphertext, signatures, credentials, and long-lived trust anchors can preserve the reward. The attacker can wait without asking the key’s permission.

The second clock

A cryptographic artifact therefore carries two clocks: the visible validity period written into a certificate or protocol, and the hidden interval before recovering its secret becomes affordable. If the artifact must remain trustworthy for years, choosing its key size silently commits you to assumptions about machines, algorithms, and adversaries that do not yet exist—and if relying systems cannot migrate, revoke, or forget that key, the commitment survives long after its designers have gone home.

Every cryptographic key size is an expiration date disguised as a number.

The nasty part is that you do not get to read the date in advance.

Design for the deadline you cannot see

“Is this key strong today?” is necessary and badly insufficient. Ask instead: how long must the protected promise survive, what remains valuable after that time, and how completely can every relying system stop trusting the key? A short-lived connection and a root certificate embedded in software archives do not deserve the same security margin, because one disappears after use while the other may keep authorizing new claims inside forgotten machines.

This is why migration machinery matters as much as impressive arithmetic. Larger keys move the economic frontier outward. Rotation, expiration, revocation, trust-store updates, algorithm agility, and the removal of obsolete clients constrain the damage when the frontier moves back. One strategy buys time; the other prevents old trust from becoming a permanent liability.

The E-Certify roots ended as curiosities because most of the ecosystem that believed them has vanished. McPherrin needed a custom old-timey TLS server because modern TLS stacks no longer share compatible protocol machinery with Netscape 4.51. The keys became recoverable, but the surrounding system had already withdrawn almost all of their power.

That is the portable test: do not ask only how hard a key is to break. Ask whether your system knows how to stop believing it.

Choose cryptography for the lifetime of the promise, then build an exit before time collects.
