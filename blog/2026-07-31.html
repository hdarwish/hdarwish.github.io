GitHub just gave your review backlog a beautiful UI

On July 30, 2026, GitHub shipped stacked pull requests into public preview. Phabricator had this in the early 2010s. Google and Meta built internal versions. Graphite, Sapling, git-machete, and a small graveyard of shell scripts named restack.sh filled the gap for everybody else. An entire industry spent a decade building tooling to work around its own code review process.

The interesting question isn't why GitHub took so long. It's why the workaround was necessary at all.

Because if you adopt stacks believing they make review faster, you will be very confused in about six weeks, when your team is shipping the same throughput with more branches, more rebases, and a brand-new genre of merge-order argument in Slack. Stacks are real and genuinely useful. They just don't do the thing the framing implies they do.

Start with what GitHub actually built, because the details are where the argument lives. You create a branch and a PR. Then you branch off that branch and open a second PR that targets the first one rather than main. Each PR shows only its own layer's diff, with a stack map at the top so a reviewer can see where their slice sits in the larger change. Reviewers work different layers in parallel. Merge the topmost ready PR and everything unmerged below it lands in one operation. Merge a lower layer alone, and the PRs above it automatically rebase and retarget. Branch protections and required checks still govern main. There's a CLI extension, gh extension install github/gh-stack, and merge queue support arriving progressively.

That auto-rebase-and-retarget line is the whole engineering achievement. It is also the tell.

Think about what it must be doing. At any moment you have several diffs standing in a line, each written against a version of the codebase that has not happened yet. Layer three assumes layer two merged. Layer two assumes layer one merged. None of that is true. They are promises about a future main, and every promise has to be re-checked and rewritten, bottom to top, whenever reality underneath it shifts. A rebase mints new commit SHAs, so the review comments anchored to the old ones are now pinned to commits that no longer exist, and the green checkmarks were computed for a tree nobody will ever merge. The automation isn't cheating. It's just doing that arithmetic for you, quietly, over and over.

Picture a factory line with one inspector at the end. Parts arrive faster than he can approve them, so they pile up on the floor in front of him. That pile is not free storage. Every part in it was machined against a spec that might be revised, and if the spec changes, somebody has to go re-machine the pile — in order, bottom to top. Manufacturing people call this work-in-progress inventory, and lean production treats it as waste rather than readiness for a precise reason: inventory hides the bottleneck by absorbing it. The line looks busy. Nothing ships sooner.

The analogy breaks in one place, and I want to be honest about where. Git parts are cheap to re-machine, sometimes free — a clean rebase costs nothing but CPU. That's exactly why the buffer is so tempting, and exactly why GitHub could automate most of it away. But the cost isn't uniform. It's convex. Independent layers rebase for nothing; layers that touch the same lines rebase into conflict, and conflict cost grows with stack depth, with time-in-queue, and with how many other people are editing those same files while you wait. You are trading a bounded, visible latency problem for an unbounded, spiky rebase-and-conflict problem. Often a fine trade. Still a trade.

Read the TED quote in GitHub's own announcement as a systems diagram rather than a testimonial. Their CTO describes AI making developers dramatically more productive, which created a new bottleneck: PRs grew large enough that reviewers struggled. Production rate went up. Inspection rate did not. So the queue in front of the inspector grew — and the queue is now the problem being solved, by making the queue a much nicer place to stand.

Every engineer who has ever branched off an unmerged branch because they couldn't afford to wait for review has already built this exact machine. By hand. In the dark. With no map and no auto-rebase. The stack is work-in-progress inventory, and it was there long before anyone drew a diagram of it.

Stacked PRs don't cure slow review. They make working around it sustainable.

Sit with that a beat, because it isn't an accusation.

Sustainable is a genuinely good thing to be. The manual version of this was miserable and error-prone and burned hours of senior-engineer attention on git rebase --onto incantations that produced precisely zero customer value. Automating a necessary workaround is honorable work — arguably the most honorable kind, since it admits the workaround exists. Landing a whole stack into a merge queue in one operation beats a week of hand-restacking. Use it. I would.

Just wire up the right instrument panel. If stacks are inventory, the number that matters is not PRs merged per week — it's how deep your stacks get and how long each layer sits before it lands. Depth is a direct readout of the gap between your production rate and your review rate. Consistently two or three deep means your review process is keeping up. Routinely seven deep, with people negotiating merge order in a thread, means you have a review bottleneck that stacking is politely concealing on your behalf. Same tool, opposite diagnosis, and the tool will never once tell you which team you are.

So watch the depth, and treat every extra layer as a bill arriving later. GitHub built you a bottleneck gauge and shipped it labeled as a productivity feature.

Buffers are how systems survive their constraints. They are never how systems escape them.
