AI Coding Tools: Slower Isn't Always Worse

I stumbled across an article that claimed AI can make coding slower. My first reaction was skeptical, having spent countless hours in code trenches where speed seemed king. But then, I thought about the trade-offs between speed and quality in my own projects.

In the past year, I've integrated AI tools into my workflow. These tools promise to enhance code quality, but they often come with a hidden cost: time. Not just the time learning the tool, but the time it takes for the AI to suggest improvements, which aren't always immediately obvious or correct.

One incident sticks with me. I was refactoring some legacy code with an AI copilot. It suggested a more elegant design pattern, and it was beautiful. However, implementing it took twice as long as just patching the old code. It involved restructuring the service layer, modifying the REST endpoints, and rewriting tests to cover edge cases the AI identified.

Speed shouldn't always be our main metric. In production systems, where uptime is sacred, slowing down for better code can feel counterintuitive. But faster isn’t always better. A slow, methodical refactor can prevent a dozen future outages.

AI tools excel at exposing you to new patterns and best practices—things that can improve code quality significantly. They suggest optimizations that might not be top of mind during a sprint. Evaluating and integrating these suggestions takes time.

The famous “move fast and break things” mantra from tech’s recent past is catchy, but we've all seen where it leads—spaghetti codebases, security holes, and technical debt that rivals the GDP of small nations.

During a major release at a previous job, we incorporated an AI-driven static analysis tool. It flagged a race condition in a goroutine that had gone unnoticed for months. Fixing it required a painstaking audit of our concurrency model. It delayed the release by two sprints, but it saved us from a potential disaster under load. Imagine a distributed system calculating finance transactions—those errors aren't just bugs; they're existential threats.

AI forces us to slow down, and that’s not always a bad thing. It’s a reminder to think critically about the code we write, rather than just shipping features. It challenges the notion that rapid feature development is the only path to success.

Not everyone is thrilled with this slowdown. I've heard grumbles from managers about AI tools dragging timelines. Speed-focused metrics can encourage cutting corners, subtly prioritizing velocity over maintainability.

We've all inherited a codebase that's an outright mess. The speed at which it was built likely contributed to the chaos. AI nudges us to build things that last.

In one project, we introduced an AI-assisted code review tool. Instead of rushing through reviews, we slowed down. Engineers engaged more deeply with the suggestions, even sparking debates over merits of various implementations. Reviews stretched longer, but our bug count dropped by 30%.

Another angle is the AI's role in onboarding new engineers. We had a junior developer who, thanks to AI tools, learned to spot anti-patterns in our microservices architecture faster than I'd ever seen before. The AI highlighted inconsistencies and potential pitfalls, sparking invaluable discussions during code reviews. The learning curve was steep, but the payoff was a more confident and capable team member who could contribute meaningfully in six months instead of twelve.

There’s a balance to strike. AI tools shouldn’t be a crutch or a bottleneck. They’re assistants, not oracles. Like any tool, they’re only as good as the craftsman wielding them.

Using AI to consciously slow down can feel against our instincts in a fast-paced industry. But in my experience, the deliberate pace leads to a codebase that isn’t just functional, but robust.

The real challenge? Shifting our perspective from viewing time spent as waste, to viewing it as investment. An investment in fewer pager duties, fewer re-writes, and fewer headaches down the road.

In a world obsessed with speed, sometimes the smarter move is to slow down.
