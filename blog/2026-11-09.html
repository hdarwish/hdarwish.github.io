The invoice isn't the product. The trust is.

When Harvest died, it wasn't the buttons that vanished. It was the unspoken promise that the tool would remain a tool.

softwarebusinesstrustsaas

Software tools are not just features; they are the physical manifestation of a social contract between a user and a vendor.

The distinctionThe difference between a product solving a problem and a product exploiting the user's dependency on that solution.

01

The Decade of Quiet Reliability

For ten years, Harvest did one thing: it tracked time and sent invoices. No blockchain for washing machines, no sudden pivot to an AI accounting platform. It was a $13.32/month subscription that solved a well-defined problem with a reliable interface. You tracked hours, generated a PDF, hit send, and closed the loop. It was the digital equivalent of a sturdy wooden workbench: it didn't change shape, it didn't ask for your biometrics, and it didn't surprise you.

This stability created a specific kind of trust. In the world of self-employment, where you are the customer support, the sales team, and the CEO, a tool that *works* is a psychological anchor. You don't just use Harvest; you build your mental model of your business around it. The chart of billed vs. paid amounts isn't just data; it's the pulse of your livelihood. When a tool becomes that load-bearing, the contract between user and vendor shifts from transactional to relational.

02

The Bending Spoons Acquisition

Then, in 2025, Bending Spoons bought Harvest. If you have watched the software industry lately, you know the name. Bending Spoons is a company famous for acquiring high-quality products, stripping them of their user-focused culture, and optimizing them for short-term revenue extraction. It happened to Vimeo. It was happening to every other asset in their portfolio. The fear wasn't that they would break the code; it was that they would break the *vibe*.

The change was swift and surgical. The price jumped from $13.32 to $133.98 per month. Everyone was moved to an "enterprise" plan with "unlimited" usage, which, as far as any user could tell, was exactly the same usage as before. The new "flex" plan at $12.65 seemed like a reprieve, but it carried a hidden tax: an additional 2% skim on top of the standard ~3% credit card fees. That 2% didn't pay for infrastructure. It paid for the privilege of sending a PDF.

03

The Death of the Human Interface

But the price hike was only the first stroke. The second was the silence. When Kyle Kingsbury wrote to Harvest to question the new billing structure, he didn't get a response from a person. He got an LLM vomiting thousands of words at him. The human loop, the part of the contract where a vendor acknowledges your existence and your specific context, was severed. You are no longer a customer; you are a ticket number in a queue that a model processes.

This is the moment the contract broke. The tool no longer served the user; the user served the tool's revenue model. The "unlimited" plan was a lie of omission, and the support system was a wall of automated noise. The grief that follows isn't about the interface. It's about the realization that your autonomy was an illusion, maintained only by the vendor's temporary lack of interest in extracting more from you.

The turn

The death of Harvest is a warning that in the post-acquisition economy, the user's trust is the last asset to be stripped, and the first to be sold.

Analogy A vendor who raises the rent on your apartment and then removes the doorman is not just a landlord; they are a squatter who has decided you don't have the right to live there.

Where it breaks It breaks because software is intangible; you can't physically lock yourself in, but the psychological lock of dependency is just as strong.

If you're deciding
When
You rely on a single SaaS vendor for a critical part of your business workflow.
Prefer
Maintain an 'exit strategy' by keeping your data in portable formats and having a backup tool identified.
Avoid
Assuming that a product's current quality is a permanent feature of its business model.

The tool is gone, but the lesson remains: your data is yours, but your trust is a loan, and the interest rate is always changing.
