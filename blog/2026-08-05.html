Your model isn't expensive. Your harness is.

Databricks took one model, held the thinking effort constant, and ran it through different agent harnesses on tasks pulled from their own multi-million-line codebase. Same model. Same effort. Quality came out flat across the harnesses. Cost per task differed by more than 2x.

That is not a result about models. It is a code review finding wearing a procurement costume.

And it comes from engineers benchmarking on the tasks they actually do, precisely because the public benchmarks have gotten saturated and gameable — which means the number is harder to wave away than most numbers in this field. If it holds, a large fraction of the "model economics" conversation you have been having with your team is misfiled. You think you are arguing about vendor pricing. You are arguing about your own plumbing.

The mechanism is mundane, in the way that expensive things usually are.

An agent does not make one call to a model. It makes a turn, then another, then another, and every single turn re-ships the whole working set: the system prompt, the tool definitions, whatever files got read, whatever tool output got pasted back in, the scaffolding instructions the harness inserts to keep the model on rails. Tokens are billed per turn, not per task. So the cost of a task is roughly context per turn multiplied by number of turns — and the harness, not the model, sets both of those numbers.

Databricks measured the first factor directly. Pi, the minimal harness in their comparison, "sent about 3x less context per turn." It ships four tools. Its system prompt plus tool definitions come in under 1,000 tokens. And it didn't only win on the multiplier — it also "kept a tighter working set and finished the tasks in fewer runs." Both factors moved the same direction at once. That is how you open a 2x cost gap while the pass rate sits perfectly still.

The genuinely surprising part is why fewer instructions produce fewer turns. More guidance should help. Instead, every instruction a harness injects competes for position against the thing the user actually asked for. Stuff enough defaults, guardrails, formatting rules, retry policies, and tool descriptions in front of a request and the request becomes one voice in a crowded room. The model burns turns rediscovering its own objective — and each of those turns re-ships the entire crowded room, at full price.

Here is the analogy I keep coming back to: a harness is not a toolbox, it is a backpack you carry on every step of the hike. A toolbox sits in the garage and costs you nothing until you open it. A backpack is charged differently — every ounce is paid for again with each step, and a heavy pack also makes you stop more often, which is more steps, which is more ounces paid. That is the compounding, and it is multiplicative, not additive. Where the analogy breaks: weight in a backpack is honest. Your shoulders tell you about it within the first mile. Prompt weight is invisible at authoring time and surfaces only as a line item at the end of the month, which is exactly why it accumulates unopposed.

The same arithmetic bites one layer down, at the model choice itself. Earendil reports that complex workflows on Haiku 4.5 often ran more expensive than on Sonnet 4.6, especially with code execution, because the cheaper model needed more turns to get there. Per-token price told you the precise opposite of the truth. Cost per completed task is the only unit with meaning in it, and cost per completed task is a property of the loop, not of the price list.

So stack it up. Context per turn is a variable your harness controls. Turn count is a variable your harness controls. Both multiply into your bill while quality holds constant. Which means the sentence "this model is too expensive for our use case" — a sentence spoken in meetings, written into RFCs, used to justify downgrading to a weaker model and eating the quality hit — has almost no content in it.

Same model, same quality, twice the bill. The harness is the variable.

Which is, if you turn it over, extraordinarily good news. The expensive-model problem is a plumbing bug, and plumbing bugs belong to you. Nobody has to ship a new frontier model for your cost per task to fall by half. Somebody just has to delete a few thousand tokens of well-meaning scaffolding that the model was already smart enough not to need.

The nativeness argument used to cover for all that scaffolding. A year ago you could plausibly claim a harness built alongside a model had structural advantages, because the model had been trained around its exact shape. That claim has weakened badly. Frontier models are now broadly competent in a terminal-style environment without being told what a terminal is — and the loudest evidence is that Anthropic cut Claude Code's system prompt by roughly 80%. When the vendor with the most native harness in existence starts aggressively deleting its own instructions, prompt-as-moat is finished.

The portable rule is a budget rule with teeth: every token in your harness must earn its keep against the fact that you pay for it on every turn of every task, forever. Not once. Forever. And minimal is not the same as weak — Shopify built pi-autoresearch as an extension, by asking Pi to read its own extension docs and write it, and got unit tests running 300 times faster and React components mounting 20% faster. None of that shipped in the box. That is the trade made correctly: nothing by default, anything on demand.

Go open your agent's system prompt. Count the tokens. Then sort them into two piles: the ones a competent model actually needed, and the ones you wrote to reassure yourself.

The cheapest agent in that benchmark wasn't the one that knew the most tricks. It was the one that got out of the way.
