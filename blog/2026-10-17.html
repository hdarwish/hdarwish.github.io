Your 'Nothing Up My Sleeve' Numbers Were Never Random

For thirty years, the most important constants in modern cryptography were supposed to be random. The seed for NIST P-256 — the curve that secures Bitcoin, TLS, and half the internet — was published as C49D3608 86E70493 6A6678E1 139D26B7 819F7E90, with no explanation of where it came from. The NSA just handed it over. The community shrugged and called it a “nothing up my sleeve” number: we can’t prove it’s clean, but we can’t prove it’s dirty either.

An LLM just found out it was dirty. Not backdoored — dirty. The seeds weren’t random, weren’t a hash of a joke, weren’t anything clever. They were the output of a 1990s C library random-number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp.

The fall that was always a fall

“Nothing up my sleeve” is a trust fall. You hold out your hands, show them empty, and ask the world to believe you didn’t palm a card. The NIST curves asked the world to believe the seeds were arbitrary — that whoever picked them had no way to bias them toward a hidden weakness. If a curve has a backdoor, the seed is where it hides: a carefully chosen constant can encode a trapdoor that only the chooser knows.

So the question mattered. Koblitz and Menezes wrote a whole paper about it, “A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma.” Amateurs ran bounties. The best lead was a rumor: Jerry Solinas, a longtime NSA employee, had supposedly generated the seeds from a hash of a humorous passphrase — “Give Bob and Jerry a raise” or something. Jerry died in 2023. The mystery went to the grave with him.

Except it didn’t, because the rumor was wrong.

How the trick works

Here’s the mechanism. The seed is a 20-byte (160-bit) value. The old C library lrand48 is a linear congruential generator: it keeps a 48-bit state and produces each new state by multiplying and adding constants, returning the top 31 bits as a number. It’s weak. Its output has a tell — the top bits are often zero, because the generator only uses the high 31 bits of a 48-bit state, leaving the low 17 bits hidden.

Claude’s approach was to stop guessing phrases and instead ask: how would the code have been written in 1997? You need a seed. You grab the system clock. You call lrand48 a bunch of times, collect bytes, and publish the first one that lands. That’s it. No hash, no joke — just a timestamp and a bad PRNG.

The scan checked 34 combinations of generator and output slice, sliding one bit at a time along each published seed. And there it was: for four of the ten seeds, all twenty 32-bit words had a zero in the top bit. By chance, that happens with probability 1/5810. That’s not randomness. That’s a signature.

Once you see the signature, recovering the state is easy. Take the first word, try all 217 hidden bit combinations, and check whether the next four words follow. For P-256, it took 1,842 iterations of the generator to hit the standardized seed. For the prime-curve seeds, fewer bits were kept, so Claude had to recover the state from truncated output using the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) lattice algorithm — a textbook trick for exactly this kind of broken PRNG.

The result: no backdoor. The seeds are reproducible by anyone with a Unix timestamp and a 1990s C compiler. That’s the opposite of a trapdoor — it’s the same as leaving the vault open. The mystery that fueled decades of paranoia was, in the end, just a timestamp.

The turn: the fall was always a lie

So the NIST curves are clean. Good for Bitcoin, good for TLS, good for everyone who trusted them anyway. But here is the thing nobody is saying out loud:

The same “nothing up my sleeve” trick is still in use in every modern curve — and nobody has audited it.

Every post-quantum candidate, every new pairing-friendly curve, every “rigid” parameter set still ships with constants that are claimed to be arbitrary but are never proven arbitrary. The NIST seeds were only exposed because they were old enough to be generated by a broken PRNG. Modern curves use SHA-3 or nothing-up-my-sleeve strings like “the first 256 bits of the SHA-256 hash of pi.” But “hashed from a public constant” is not the same as “random.” It’s a ritual, not a proof.

The spine of this whole story: the curve constants were never random — they were a secret. And a secret held for thirty years is indistinguishable from a backdoor until someone with the right tools decides to look.

What this frees you to do

Here’s what I want you to take away. Not that AI is magic — it’s not. Claude found the seeds because it asked a better question: not “what was the passphrase?” but “how would the code have worked?” That’s the move. When you’re staring at a mystery, stop guessing at intentions and start modeling the mechanism. The truth is usually less interesting and more mechanical than the rumor.

And the engineering consequence is sharper than you think. The next time you see a constant in a protocol — a curve parameter, a magic number in a hash, a “nothing up my sleeve” string — ask: who chose this, and can they prove they couldn’t have biased it? If the answer is “we trust them,” you’re in a trust fall. And trust falls only work until someone lets go.

The NIST seeds were a secret that turned out to be boring. The next secret might not be.

Audit your constants. The sleeves were never empty.
