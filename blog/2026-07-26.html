Your autoscaler is not slow. It's an oscillator.

A car dealer does everything right and ends up in a permanent boom-bust cycle.

She wants her lot to hold ten days of sales. Demand sits at 20 cars a day, jumps to 22, spikes once to 70, then flattens out and stays flat forever. She's cautious — she doesn't want to panic-order hundreds of cars off one weird Tuesday — so she adopts the sensible rule: each day, order only half the gap between what she has and what she wants. Deliveries take five days, because deliveries take five days. Nobody in this story is careless, greedy, or bad at arithmetic.

Her inventory never settles. It swings, forever, on constant demand. This is Martin Janiczek's worked simulation of the delay chapter from Thinking in Systems, and it should ruin a belief you probably hold.

The belief is this: that a system which overshoots and undershoots is a system that isn't reacting hard enough or fast enough. Every instinct you have about autoscalers, retry budgets, alert thresholds, and how quickly a human should respond to a graph is built on it. Tighten the loop. Shorten the interval. Be more responsive. That instinct is aimed at the wrong dial, and in the lagged case it doesn't merely fail — it makes things worse.

Watch the gears. Day one, demand ticks up to 22. Desired inventory is sales × 10, so the target moves from 200 to 220. Gap: 20 cars. She's prudent, so she orders 10. Those ten cars do not exist yet. They exist as a promise — an order in flight, invisible on the lot, arriving in five days.

Day two, she looks at the lot. The gap is still there, because of course it is; nothing has arrived. She orders half of it again. Day three, same. Day four, same. Day five, same. By the time the first shipment lands she has placed five overlapping orders, each one sized as if the previous four had never happened.

That's the whole mechanism. The pipeline is a stock she isn't counting.

Then the shipments start landing, one per day, and now she has too many cars. The gap flips sign. She stops ordering entirely and waits for sales to eat the surplus. Sales do eat the surplus — and then keep eating, straight past the target, because there is now nothing in the pipeline to catch the fall. She's short again. She starts ordering again. Five days of blindness again.

Here is the analogy that carries the load, and I want to be careful with it. It's a shower on the end of a long pipe. You turn the knob; nothing changes; you turn it further; still nothing; you turn it further still — and then thirty seconds of increasingly scalding water arrives all at once and you leap sideways and crank it cold, which will also arrive in thirty seconds. The oscillation is not caused by your poor taste in temperature. It's caused by the pipe. Where the analogy breaks: in the shower, you eventually learn the pipe and hold still with your hand off the knob. The dealer can't learn, because her rule is fixed — and neither can your autoscaler, because you wrote its rule down in YAML.

So the fix looks obvious. Stop damping. Set the response divisor to 1: see a gap of 30, order 30, close it today, be maximally responsive. The delivery delay is physical and can't be shortened, but the response delay is a knob you own, and it's the timid knob, so surely turning it to full aggression trades a little spike-sensitivity for stability.

It doesn't. Reacting at full strength inside a lagged loop makes the swings bigger. You now order the entire phantom gap five times over before any of it arrives, so you overshoot harder, so you starve longer waiting for the surplus to clear, so the next trough runs deeper. Faster response is not a smaller error. It's a bigger amplitude at a shorter period.

Control theory has a name for what's happening: the correction arrives with a phase shift. Your action is a response to an error that has already aged, and if it lands late enough it stops opposing the error and starts reinforcing whatever the error has since become. Negative feedback with enough lag is positive feedback wearing a lanyard. Cranking the gain doesn't fix the phase; it just multiplies whatever the phase is doing to you.

And the thing worth sitting with is that the dealer never makes a mistake. Not one. Every single day, given what she can see, she takes the most defensible action available — the prudent, half-measure, don't-panic action that any of us would praise in a code review — and the aggregate of all those reasonable actions is a machine that cannot stop shaking. The oscillation isn't hers. It belongs to the loop. And it is the same loop shape as your autoscaler polling a metric that lags by two scrape intervals, your retry budget reacting to timeouts that are themselves sitting in a queue, your capacity plan built on last quarter's growth.

In a lagged feedback loop, reacting faster makes the system oscillate, not stabilize.

Which has a consequence people skip past: no amount of better judgment inside the loop can fix a loop with the wrong shape. Hire a sharper manager, write a cleverer scaling policy, put your most experienced engineer on the graph — and if the delay is still there and still uncounted, you get the identical waveform with better-worded justifications for each swing. That's why "be more responsive" is such a seductive dead end. It feels like effort. It's a longer lever on the same broken linkage.

What actually helps is boring and structural. Count the pipeline: an order in flight is inventory you already own, a scale-up already dispatched is capacity you already have, and a controller that can't see in-flight work is guessing with confidence. Or take the lag seriously and damp past the point of comfort, because a system reacting to a five-day pipe on a one-day cadence isn't diligent, it's loud. Janiczek says he'd like to know what mathematically makes the oscillation inevitable — that's exactly the right curiosity, and phase margin is sitting there waiting for him. But you don't need the math to act on the shape.

So the next time a graph sawtooths at you, resist the urge to sharpen the response. Ask instead what the controller is looking at, and how old that information is by the time it arrives.

Most of the time, you are not too slow. You are shouting into a pipe and startling yourself with the echo.
